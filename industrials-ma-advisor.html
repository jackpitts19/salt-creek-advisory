Industrials & Manufacturing

Thinking about selling
your manufacturing business?

Industrials is the busiest category in the lower middle market right now, and that cuts both ways: more buyers chasing you, and buyers who have gotten precise about what they will pay for. It comes down to a few things: earnings that survive the maintenance capex line, customers who are papered and qualified, and a plant that runs when you are not on the floor. Most owners have more of that story than they think, and the ones who do not can build it. At Salt Creek Advisory, we help you get it in order, then put enough of the right buyers in the room that they have to compete for it. Both of us on every call, and nothing owed until it closes.

The 2026 Industrials Market

One of the widest buyer fields in the market. Every one of them does the math.

A serious buyer will take your EBITDA apart and rebuild it from the floor up: what it costs to keep the equipment running, which customers are papered and qualified, what is left in the fleet. Your headline multiple is where they start, not where they land. Here is the market they are working from.

There is no single manufacturing multiple.
The number follows the cash, and the structure decides what you keep.

Activity is not spread evenly. Ranked by activity in Axial's May 2026 industrials survey, HVAC led by a wide margin, followed by construction and engineering, electrical equipment and components, machinery and equipment, energy, and aerospace and defense, with the most buyer attention on data center cooling, power infrastructure, electrical contracting, residential service trades, automation, and aerospace and defense manufacturing. A certified aerospace machining shop and a general job shop running comparable equipment are not the same business, and they do not sell for the same price. What moves you up the range is evidence: earnings that survive the maintenance capex line, customers who are papered and qualified, equipment with life left in it, and a plant that runs without you on the floor.

What Earns a Premium

Premiums are earned
below the EBITDA line.

These are the six things buyers dig into once they get past your reported earnings. We help you get each one ready before we ever go to market, which is also most of what buyers look for in any acquisition target.

01

Cash EBITDA

Reported EBITDA minus a three-year average of maintenance capex. Broker-published intensities run 3% to 10% of revenue by process, from general machine shops to medical device work. Buyers model this in the first week.

02

Certifications That Gate Work

AS9100, Nadcap, ISO 13485, IATF 16949, ITAR registration. Each decides what you are permitted to quote, and requalifying a replacement supplier costs your customer an audit, a first article inspection, and a validation run.

03

Agreements, Not Blanket POs

A blanket purchase order carries no binding volume. A long-term agreement has a term, often minimum volumes, and change-of-control language a buyer's counsel will read first. Know which one you actually hold.

04

Customer Concentration

Under 15% from a single customer earns no discount. From 25% to 40% draws a broker-published 0.5 to 1.5 turn discount plus earnout or escrow conversations. We flag it early so it does not surprise you at the table.

05

Equipment With Life Left

A fleet averaging under ten years old with 70% or better utilization is the broker-published marker for platform pricing. An auditable asset register and an independent appraisal also decide how much a buyer can borrow to pay you.

06

Runs Without You

If technical talent, production know-how, or customer relationships sit with one or two people, buyers price a transition risk. A management bench that runs the plant when you are not in it is what makes the price real.

What we actually do
for a manufacturing owner.

01

Rebuild EBITDA on a Cash Basis

We pull three years of maintenance capex, separate it from growth spending, and put cash EBITDA next to reported EBITDA before a buyer does. The multiple gets applied to the number that survives, so it has to be yours first.

02

Paper the Durability

We sort long-term agreements from blanket purchase orders, confirm certifications and audit histories are current, size customer concentration against the published thresholds, and age the inventory so the working capital peg is negotiated from your numbers rather than a diligence provider's corrections.

A Phase II environmental finding and multiemployer pension withdrawal liability end deals rather than repricing them. If your site or your plan carries either, the Phase I and the withdrawal liability estimate come first, well before launch, with time to resolve what they find.

Who Is Buying

There are plenty of buyers. No two underwrite you the same way.

A strategic acquirer and a private equity platform can price the same plant very differently, because they are paying for different things. Fit, integration, rollover, and your role after close can outweigh the headline. It helps to know how strategic buyers and private equity buyers think differently before you pick one.

Strategic Acquirers

52.4% of industrials deal flow in 2025, per Capstone Partners. A strategic can delete duplicate overhead, push your parts through its own sales channel, absorb your volume into a plant running below capacity, or buy your certifications to reach a customer it cannot quote today. That is value a sponsor structurally cannot pay for.

Private Equity Platforms

47.6% of deal flow, the highest sponsor share on Capstone's record. Platforms buy controlling stakes in precision, specialty, and contract manufacturers, keep management in place, and add capabilities through bolt-ons. A sponsor underwrites your standalone cash flow, so its ceiling is what the business supports roughly as it runs today.

Permanent Holders

Marmon Holdings, a Berkshire Hathaway company, now comprises more than 120 autonomous businesses in 11 groups. It began as a family business in 1953 and was sold to Berkshire in 2008. Its model, buying specialized manufacturers and leaving them to run, appeals to owners who want things kept as they are.

Regional Strategics & Independent Sponsors

Larger industrial companies adding capacity, geographic footprint, or a specialized capability, and occasionally a family office or independent sponsor building a platform. With manufacturing capacity utilization at 75.7% in June 2026, per the Federal Reserve’s G.17 release, a buyer with slack has a stronger case for absorbing your volume than for paying to keep a second plant running.

Cash EBITDA is reported EBITDA minus a three-year average of maintenance capital expenditure, the spending that keeps existing capacity running rather than adding new capacity. Reported EBITDA adds depreciation back as a non-cash entry, and in a plant that add-back is close to a fiction: the presses, spindles, compressors, and roofs wear out and must be replaced. A plant doing $3 million of EBITDA with $600,000 of average maintenance capex has $2.4 million of cash EBITDA. A buyer offering 6x on reported EBITDA pays $18 million, and the term sheet will accurately say 6.0x, but divide that $18 million by $2.4 million and the buyer is really paying 7.5x. Applied to cash EBITDA instead, 6x would have produced $14.4 million, a $3.6 million difference behind one identical-sounding multiple. Private equity buyers build this into their model in the first week of diligence, and a well-advised strategic does too. Run it on your own numbers before someone runs it on you; our guide to quality of earnings reports covers how a diligence team formalizes it.

We have not found data supporting the idea that owning your facility raises the multiple on the operating business, and we would be suspicious of a source claiming it. The two assets get priced separately. Where ownership genuinely moves your outcome is rent normalization. If your plant would rent for $400,000 a year and you have been charging the operating company $150,000, a buyer imputes the $250,000 difference before applying any multiple, which at 6x removes $1.5 million of enterprise value before anyone discusses the building itself. The reverse holds too: an above-market related-party rent is an add-back buyers accept when the market comparison is documented. Selling the real estate with the business, retaining it on an arm's-length lease, or running a sale-leaseback alongside the deal each produce different total proceeds and different tax outcomes, so get a broker's opinion of market rent early.
