    Direct Answer

    Most privately held businesses sell for a multiple of their annual profit. Larger ones are priced on EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization) after paying someone a market salary to run the business. Smaller ones, which usually sell to someone who will run them, are priced on seller’s discretionary earnings (SDE): profit with the owner’s own pay still in it. This calculator uses SDE below $300,000 of owner earnings, EBITDA from $750,000, and a blend of the two in between. It starts from the published multiple range for your industry and subsector, then moves the multiple up or down for size, owner involvement, growth, customer concentration, and how dependable your revenue is. The result is a range, not a price. Where you land inside it depends mostly on whether buyers compete for your business.

    How this business valuation calculator works

    The calculator follows the same order a buyer’s math does, and the result screen shows you every step with your own numbers.

    1. Start with your profit. If you leave it blank, we estimate it from your revenue and typical margins for your subsector.
    2. Work out your owner earnings. That is your profit with your own pay in it (SDE). If you already took your pay out, we add a typical owner’s salary back. If you leave profit blank and revenue is under $1 million, we estimate it from typical margins plus a typical salary, capped at 35% of revenue.
    3. Pick the method. Below $300,000 of owner earnings, a business usually sells to someone who will run it, so we price the SDE itself. From $750,000, buyers usually hire a manager, so we set aside a market salary for whoever replaces you, scaled to company size from $65,000 under $1 million of revenue up to $200,000 at $15 million and above, and price what is left as EBITDA. In between, we blend the two in proportion, so there is no cliff where the method changes.
    4. Pick the starting multiple. We use the published multiple range for your industry and subsector, on SDE, EBITDA, or both. The low end reflects a typical negotiated sale; the high end, a strong business in a competitive process. Where these come from is listed below.
    5. Adjust for your business. Both ends move up or down for your size, owner involvement, growth, largest customer, and revenue type. On the SDE path each answer counts for 40% of its full weight, because SDE multiples are smaller numbers. For a profitable business the tool never goes below 2.0x at the low end or 2.5x at the high end on EBITDA, or 1.0x and 1.5x on SDE.
    6. Multiply and round. Earnings times each end of the multiple, rounded to the nearest $25,000 under $1 million, $100,000 under $10 million, and $500,000 above that, so the range never looks more precise than an estimate can be. We show the midpoint too.

    Your state, the timing question and the real estate question do not change the number. Very large businesses, with about $12 million or more of profit or $120 million or more of revenue, get a conversation instead of a number, because a calculator range is not useful at that scale.

    SDE vs. EBITDA: which number is yours?

    If your profit figure still includes what you pay yourself, it is seller’s discretionary earnings (SDE), the number smaller businesses are usually priced on. A business that size usually sells to someone who will run it, so the buyer is paying for everything the business pays its owner. Larger businesses usually sell to buyers who will hire a manager, so they are priced on EBITDA, after a market salary for that manager. That is why the tool asks whether your pay is still in the number: it decides which figure we start from.

    The difference is not a technicality. A multiple of SDE and a multiple of EBITDA are different units: 3x of one is not 3x of the other. In the example above, the owner’s $900,000 includes their pay and puts the shop on the EBITDA path, so a $115,000 salary comes out first. Treating the $900,000 as if it were already EBITDA would have produced $4.0 million to $6.2 million, about $600,000 too high at the low end and $800,000 at the high end. Below $300,000 of owner earnings the tool prices SDE alone, and between $300,000 and $750,000 it blends the two methods, leaning further toward EBITDA as earnings grow. For more, see EBITDA and business valuation basics and which EBITDA add-backs hold up.

    What a calculator can’t see

    A range from eight answers leaves out most of what decides a real price. The biggest gaps:

    • Add-backs. One-time and owner-specific costs that a buyer may agree to add back to your profit. See EBITDA add-backs.
    • Quality of earnings. Whether your numbers hold up when a buyer’s accountants test them. See what a quality of earnings report finds.
    • Working capital. How much cash, receivables and inventory have to stay in the business at closing. See the working capital peg.
    • Deal structure. How much is paid at closing and how much later, or only if targets are hit. See M&A deal structure and earnouts, escrow, and holdbacks.
    • What you keep. Debt, taxes and fees come out of the price. See what you keep after selling a business.
    • Whether buyers compete. The single biggest swing between the low end and the high end. See how a competitive sale process works.
