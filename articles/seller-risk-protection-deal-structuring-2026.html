How Sellers Protect
Themselves After Closing

Escrow, indemnification caps and baskets, survival periods, and representations and warranties insurance are negotiated as one package. Each one changes how much of the price you actually keep.

Direct Answer

Four mechanisms decide how much of a sale price stays at risk after closing. An escrow holds part of the proceeds with a third party, an indemnification cap limits the seller’s total recoverable losses, a basket sets the threshold a claim must cross, and a survival period sets the deadline for bringing one. Buyer-side representations and warranties insurance can replace much of the escrow for covered unknown breaches, which is why the median general indemnification escrow runs 10% of transaction value without that insurance and 0.5% with it. Negotiate the four together rather than one at a time, because the headline purchase price does not show how much of it you keep.

TL;DR
  • Sellers and buyers negotiate escrow, caps and baskets, survival periods, and R&W insurance as one package because each term changes the seller’s remaining exposure.
  • Traditional escrow arrangements often hold 10% to 20% of the purchase price for 12 to 24 months, although transaction risk can change both terms.
  • An indemnification cap limits recoverable losses, a basket establishes the claim threshold, and a survival period sets the deadline for claims. Fraud and specified fundamental representations often receive different treatment.
  • Buyer-side R&W insurance can replace part of the seller’s escrow for covered, unknown breaches. Policies generally exclude known issues, covenants, and purchase price adjustments. Insurers may also pursue sellers responsible for fraud.

Key Takeaways

  • Sellers can seek more cash at closing when an insurer gives the buyer another source of recovery for covered claims.
  • Comparing proposals from multiple buyers helps you assess post-closing exposure alongside price and rollover equity.
  • A low indemnification cap may offer limited protection if broad carve-outs expose the seller to additional claims.
  • R&W insurance shifts specified risks toward the insurer, but policy exclusions and the retention still require careful negotiation.

What Happens to Sale Proceeds After Closing

A signed purchase price does not always equal the cash you receive at closing. A buyer may require part of the proceeds to remain unavailable to cover obligations or claims that may arise after ownership transfers.

Indemnification provides the broader framework for allocating that post-closing risk. The purchase agreement specifies which losses the seller may have to cover, along with the applicable thresholds, limits, and claim periods. Potential claims may involve inaccurate statements about the business, unpaid taxes, breached covenants, or post-closing purchase price adjustments.

You should evaluate an offer based on cash at closing and your remaining exposure. Review how much money stays restricted and when you can receive it. Then determine whether the buyer can pursue additional recovery. Two offers with the same headline price can produce different proceeds and risk after closing, which is the same problem the six components of deal structure create at the front end.

Escrow Holdbacks

Escrow definition. An escrow places part of the purchase price with an independent third party after closing. The escrow agent releases those funds according to written instructions in the purchase agreement and escrow agreement.

An escrow differs from a holdback. With an escrow, a bank or trust company usually holds the money. With a holdback, the buyer keeps the unpaid amount. Escrow gives the seller the protection of third-party control and reduces reliance on the buyer’s ability to pay later, a distinction covered further in Salt Creek’s guide to earnouts, escrows, and holdbacks.

R&W insurance can substantially reduce the general indemnification escrow. The median general indemnification escrow was 10% of transaction value for deals without R&W insurance, compared with 0.5% when the parties used that insurance. A separate escrow may still cover known issues or purchase price adjustments that the policy excludes.

Holding periods commonly run 12 to 24 months after closing. The escrow agreement sets the requirements for claim notices and disputes. It also governs permitted investments and the release schedule. Some agreements release part of the money after six or twelve months if no claims remain outstanding.

Escrowed funds generally go to the seller when the agreed period ends, except for amounts tied to valid or unresolved claims. Sellers should confirm whether a pending claim freezes only the disputed amount or delays the entire release.

Indemnification Caps and Baskets

An indemnification cap limits the seller’s total liability for covered claims after closing. If covered losses reach $3 million but the cap is $2 million, the seller generally owes no more than $2 million. The purchase agreement specifies which claims count toward that limit.

Caps usually cover breaches of representations and warranties, which are factual statements the seller makes about the company. ABA study data summarized by Morse found that 86% of deals set caps below the purchase price, and almost 40% set them between 1% and 10%. Most deals with R&W insurance used caps below 1%.

An indemnification basket sets the loss threshold a buyer must cross before seeking payment. A deductible basket makes the seller responsible only for losses above the threshold. With a $1 million deductible and $1.5 million of covered losses, the seller owes $500,000.

A tipping basket works differently. Once losses exceed the threshold, the buyer can recover the full amount. With a $1 million tipping basket and $1.5 million of covered losses, the seller may owe the entire $1.5 million. The ABA study found that 67% of baskets used the deductible structure. Most baskets equaled 1% or less of transaction value, and 56% equaled 0.5% or less.

The stated cap and basket apply only to the claim categories covered by those limits. Deals often exclude fraud and fundamental representations covering matters such as the seller’s authority, ownership, and capitalization. Fraud fell outside the general cap in 90% of studied deals, while due organization and due authority were excluded in 74% and 73%, respectively. A seller should therefore review each liability category rather than treating one headline cap as the maximum exposure for every claim.

Survival Periods

A survival period is the time after closing when a buyer may bring a claim for breach of a seller’s representation or warranty. Once the applicable period expires, the buyer generally cannot pursue that claim, subject to the purchase agreement and governing law.

General representations often survive for 12 to 18 months. These cover routine matters such as contracts, employees, customers, and financial information. One ABA study summary found that 90% of reviewed deals used 18 months or less for nonfundamental representations.

Fundamental representations usually survive longer because they address the seller’s authority to complete the sale and ownership of the shares. Agreements may provide survival periods of three to six years or longer, and the standard cap and basket may not apply. Some fundamental representations may survive indefinitely, as described in guidance on representations and warranties.

Tax representations often follow the applicable tax statute of limitations rather than a fixed period. Fraud claims commonly sit outside contractual caps, and their timing depends on the agreement and governing law.

The survival period for general representations often determines when the parties release the general indemnification escrow. A shorter period can return escrowed proceeds sooner, while longer periods can keep seller funds or direct liability exposed for longer. Legal counsel should review each category separately because one expiration date rarely governs every claim.

How Representations and Warranties Insurance Works

Representations and warranties insurance, or R&W insurance, covers losses caused by unknown breaches of the seller’s statements in the purchase agreement. Those statements may address financial records, taxes, contracts, ownership, and other facts about the business.

Most R&W policies insure the buyer. After discovering a covered breach, the buyer submits its claim directly to the insurer rather than pursuing the seller for the full loss. A buy-side policy can reduce or eliminate the traditional indemnification escrow, which allows the seller to receive more cash at closing. A smaller seller escrow may still cover the seller’s negotiated responsibility for losses within the policy retention.

R&W insurance costs include a premium, a retention, and an underwriting fee. Published R&W insurance pricing guidance puts the premium at roughly 3% of the coverage amount, with policy limits of about 10% of transaction value and a retention of about 0.5% to 1% of transaction value. Underwriting fees run about $25,000 to $50,000, and many insurers set a minimum premium of about $100,000 to $150,000. That guidance also notes that premiums have been falling, and pricing below 3% is no longer uncommon.

Fixed fees and minimum premiums make R&W insurance proportionally more expensive for smaller transactions, although smaller deals can still qualify. The buyer and seller negotiate who bears the premium, retention, and related fees.

R&W insurance does not cover every post-closing dispute. Policies commonly exclude issues discovered or disclosed during due diligence, covenant breaches, forward-looking promises, and purchase-price or working-capital adjustments. Certain environmental, employee-benefit, and cybersecurity risks may also require separate underwriting or remain excluded, according to policy coverage guidance.

R&W insurance also does not shield a seller from fraud. Insurers generally preserve the right to pursue a seller who committed fraud, even when the buyer otherwise claims against the policy. You should treat R&W insurance as protection against unknown breaches rather than a substitute for accurate disclosure and careful legal review.

How the Four Mechanisms Trade Off Against Each Other

Together, escrow, the indemnification cap and basket, survival periods, and R&W insurance allocate post-closing risk between the buyer, seller, and insurer. You should compare the full package because a favorable term may offset a less favorable one. For example, a seller might accept a larger cap in exchange for a shorter survival period and earlier escrow release.

R&W insurance gives the buyer another source of recovery for covered, unknown breaches and can reduce the seller’s direct exposure. According to a summary of the 2024 M&A Deal Terms Study, insured deals were less likely to provide for contractual survival of representations and warranties and had lower caps on average.

In an insured deal that retains seller indemnification, the purchase agreement’s basket determines when the seller becomes liable, while the policy retention determines when the insurer begins paying covered losses. All R&W-insured deals reviewed in one ABA study used deductible-style baskets, which leave losses below the threshold with the buyer rather than making the seller responsible from the first dollar once claims cross it. Most insured deals also had caps below 1% of purchase price, according to a summary of the ABA findings.

Compare the insurance cost with the amount of additional cash the seller receives at closing. If the seller pays part of the premium but receives substantially more cash at closing, the added liquidity may justify the expense. Known issues and purchase price adjustments may still require separate escrows or indemnities. Before comparing proposals, ask legal counsel to identify each policy exclusion and the party responsible for the excluded loss.

Questions to Ask Before Agreeing to Terms

  • How much of the purchase price will remain in escrow, and when will the escrow agent release it?
  • Which claims can exceed the indemnification cap, including claims involving fraud, taxes, or fundamental representations?
  • Does the basket operate like a deductible, or does crossing the threshold make the full claim recoverable?
  • How long does each representation survive after closing, and does that period match the escrow term?
  • Who can make a claim, what evidence must the buyer provide, and how can the seller dispute it?
  • Could R&W insurance reduce the escrow, cap, or survival period?
  • Who will pay the R&W insurance premium, underwriting fee, retention, and related expenses?
  • Which known issues, covenants, and exclusions will remain the seller’s responsibility despite the policy?
  • Do competing buyers offer meaningfully different post-closing terms, even if their headline prices look similar?
  • How much cash could remain exposed after closing under the proposed package?

Getting the Right Terms for Your Sale

Before accepting an offer, compare its cash at closing with the amount, duration, and categories of your remaining exposure. Your advisers can then determine whether concessions on one term are offset by stronger protection elsewhere.
