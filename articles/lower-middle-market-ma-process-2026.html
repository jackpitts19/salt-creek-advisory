What Is the Lower Middle Market
M&A Process?

Selling a business involves more moving parts than most owners expect: here is how a typical lower middle market sell-side process unfolds from start to finish.

Direct Answer

The sell-side M&A process is a structured effort to prepare your company, approach qualified buyers confidentially, compare offers, negotiate terms, complete due diligence, and close a sale. A typical lower-middle-market transaction takes about six to nine months, though complex diligence, financing, or legal issues can extend the schedule beyond a year. A competitive process helps establish market pricing and compare deal terms. Preparation and process design give you more control over a business sale.

TL;DR
  • A lower-middle-market sale commonly takes six to nine months, though complex deals can run past a year. You can reduce avoidable delays by preparing accurate financial records and supporting adjusted EBITDA before outreach begins.
  • The process moves through preparation, buyer research, confidential outreach, initial offers, management meetings, letter of intent negotiation, due diligence, and closing.
  • A competitive process helps establish market pricing and compare deal terms. In Q1 2026, 83% of deals above $5 million received at least three offers.
  • Advisor fees vary by deal size and engagement model. Advisors may charge retainers, monthly fees, success fees, or a combination, so you should compare total expected cost and payment timing.

What the Sell-Side M&A Process Is and How Long It Takes

The sell-side M&A process is a structured effort to prepare your company, approach qualified buyers confidentially, compare offers, negotiate terms, complete due diligence, and close a sale. A typical lower-middle-market transaction takes about six to nine months, though complex diligence, financing, or legal issues can extend the schedule beyond a year.

Your advisor prepares the company for buyers before outreach begins. Your advisor reviews financial records, calculates adjusted EBITDA, and develops marketing materials. Adjusted EBITDA estimates normalized operating earnings after accounting for interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization, and defensible owner-specific or one-time expenses.

Your advisor then contacts qualified buyers confidentially and manages the offer process. Interested buyers submit initial indications of interest, and selected buyers meet management before presenting letters of intent. After you sign a letter of intent, the chosen buyer conducts financial, legal, tax, and operational diligence. Both sides then negotiate the purchase agreement and closing terms.

The phase-by-phase timelines in this guide use that sequence as their reference point. Your company’s readiness, buyer response, deal structure, and record quality will determine where the schedule expands or contracts.

This guide addresses owners of established private companies with roughly $2 million to $75 million in annual revenue and generally at least $500,000 of adjusted EBITDA. It applies to founders and families considering a sale soon, evaluating an unsolicited offer, or preparing several years in advance.

Preparing the Business and Building Marketing Materials

Buyer preparation begins with financial records that can withstand buyer scrutiny. Your advisor will reconcile tax returns, income statements, and balance sheets while separating recurring revenue from one-time activity. Buyers may also request revenue and profitability by customer, service line, or location, based on the units relevant to the company.

Adjusted EBITDA gives buyers a clearer view of ongoing earnings. EBITDA measures earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. Adjustments may remove a one-time legal expense or normalize owner compensation, but each adjustment needs documentation and a reasonable business explanation. Buyers will discount adjustments that appear aggressive or lack support.

The marketing package turns the company’s records into a clear investment case. A short teaser describes the business without revealing its identity and allows prospective buyers to decide whether to sign a nondisclosure agreement. After signing, qualified buyers receive a confidential information memorandum. The memorandum explains the company’s operations and market position, then supports its growth case with financial performance and operating data.

A secure data room holds the documents behind those claims. You should expect to gather financial statements, major contracts, employment information, and other records that buyers will eventually review. If you resolve inconsistencies before outreach, buyers ask fewer follow-up questions and lowers the chance that a buyer changes its valuation during due diligence.

Preparation often controls the broader sale timeline because incomplete records delay marketing and weaken buyer confidence. Your advisor should test the materials as a buyer would. Your advisor should address unsupported forecasts and any concentration or owner-dependence concerns before buyers begin forming views on value and risk.

Salt Creek Advisory uses proprietary technology and AI to organize company information and flag inconsistencies during initial buyer research during preparation. Jack and Connor Pitts review the output and remain responsible for financial interpretation, positioning, confidentiality, and judgment. Technology can reduce manual work, but an advisor still must decide which facts matter and how buyers are likely to interpret them.

Building the Buyer List and Running Confidential Outreach

A sell-side M&A advisor builds the buyer list around fit rather than volume. The advisor evaluates each buyer's industry focus, transaction size, acquisition history, geography, and ability to finance a deal. You should review the list and exclude competitors or other parties whose involvement could compromise confidentiality.

Different buyer types value your company for different reasons. Strategic buyers may see value in your customers, capabilities, or market position. Private equity firms typically evaluate your company as a new platform investment or an addition to an existing portfolio company, and they made about one-fifth of lower-middle-market acquisitions in 2025, according to IBBA and M&A Source Market Pulse data. Family offices invest capital for wealthy families, while independent sponsors raise funding for a specific acquisition. Individual buyers, including first-time buyers and serial entrepreneurs, made up 44% of lower-middle-market acquisitions in the same period, so they remain a buyer type worth including in most processes.

A targeted process approaches selected buyers privately instead of posting the business on a marketplace or distributing materials to an unfiltered list. Salt Creek uses buyer research technology to expand coverage and organize outreach, but Jack and Connor review buyer fit and manage communications. A broader list can create competition, but outreach should remain limited to credible buyers who have a reason to pursue the company.

Confidentiality practices control what each buyer sees and when they see it. Initial outreach uses a blind teaser that describes the business without naming it or revealing identifying details. Interested buyers must sign a nondisclosure agreement before receiving the confidential information memorandum. The advisor can release sensitive records such as customer names and detailed contracts later as buyers demonstrate serious interest.

Buyer outreach and initial screening often take four to eight weeks. During that period, the advisor handles buyer responses and nondisclosure agreements, including preliminary questions. The advisor then identifies buyers prepared to submit an indication of interest. No process can eliminate confidentiality risk, but staged disclosure, controlled data-room access, and documented communications reduce unnecessary exposure.

Indications of Interest, Management Meetings, and the Letter of Intent

The middle phase usually takes six to ten weeks after buyers receive the confidential information memorandum. Your advisor collects indications of interest and uses them to choose candidates for management meetings and final letter of intent proposals.

An indication of interest, or IOI, gives a buyer’s preliminary valuation and proposed transaction structure. Most IOIs remain nonbinding and rely on assumptions that the buyer has not yet verified. Your advisor should compare each proposal’s total economics and closing conditions rather than ranking buyers by headline price alone.

Management meetings give shortlisted buyers direct access to you and other senior leaders. Buyers use these discussions to assess management and confirm their view of the company and its growth prospects. You can also evaluate how each buyer makes decisions and how its plans would affect you and your employees.

After management meetings, serious buyers submit updated proposals or letters of intent. An LOI records the proposed price and explains how the buyer plans to pay it. A strong LOI also addresses working capital assumptions, treatment of debt and cash, expected diligence, financing conditions, required approvals, and the proposed closing schedule. Resolve major economic points in the LOI and limit the buyer’s ability to change its offer after diligence. or gives the buyer broad rights to change its offer after diligence.

Exclusivity deserves careful review because it changes your negotiating position. Once you sign an LOI with an exclusivity provision, you generally agree to stop soliciting or negotiating with other buyers for a stated period. The selected buyer can then complete diligence without competition from the other bidders.

Before signing, you should resolve as many material terms as possible while multiple buyers still remain engaged. Your M&A advisor can compare the commercial proposals, while your transaction attorney reviews binding provisions such as exclusivity and confidentiality. A clear LOI cannot eliminate closing risk, but it can reduce disputes over terms that both sides could have addressed earlier.

How a Competitive Process Compares With a Single Unsolicited Offer

In the Q1 2026 Market Pulse survey, 83% of deals over $5 million attracted at least three offers, and 18% attracted ten or more bids. Multiple offers are common in lower-middle-market transactions, although no process can guarantee them.

Competition strengthens your negotiating position because each buyer knows you have credible alternatives. A defined bid deadline encourages buyers to submit serious terms earlier. If one buyer later reduces its price or adds a difficult condition, you may still have other parties to call.

Multiple bids also reveal how the market values your company. A strategic buyer may pay for cost savings or access to customers that it can gain through an acquisition. A private equity firm may value the same company based on expected growth and the returns available under its ownership model. One unsolicited offer reflects one buyer’s analysis, not a full market test.

A competitive process also gives you more transaction structures to compare. One buyer may offer more cash at closing, while another proposes rollover equity or an earnout that could increase your eventual proceeds. Buyers may also differ in closing certainty and post-closing plans. Your advisor should compare the economic value and risk of each proposal rather than rank offers by headline price alone.

You may still want to explore an unsolicited offer. Before granting exclusivity, you can test whether other qualified buyers have interest through a controlled and confidential outreach process. A limited market check provides a benchmark while limiting unnecessary disclosure.

Due Diligence, Quality of Earnings, and Working Capital

Due diligence usually runs for six to ten weeks after you sign the letter of intent, although weak records or complex deal terms can extend it. During this period, the buyer verifies the information you provided before signing the LOI and investigates issues that could affect valuation or closing. The buyer often conducts several workstreams at once while attorneys negotiate the purchase agreement.

A quality of earnings review, commonly called a QoE, examines how the company generates profit and whether adjusted EBITDA reflects recurring performance. The buyer’s accounting firm tests revenue and expenses, including proposed earnings adjustments. Document each adjustment and reconcile financial results with the marketing materials to preserve the agreed valuation and deal terms.

Working capital equals short-term operating assets minus short-term operating liabilities. The purchase agreement defines the included categories. Buyers commonly expect the company to deliver a normal level of working capital at closing so the business can continue operating without an immediate cash contribution. A working capital true-up compares the estimated closing balance against an agreed target and adjusts the purchase price after final figures become available. The target should account for seasonality and the company’s historical operating needs.

You should expect a significant documentation and coordination burden during diligence. Buyers may request detailed financial records, including monthly statements and tax returns. They will also examine customer contracts and records for insurance and employment. Your M&A advisor can coordinate with your accountant and attorney to manage requests and resolve inconsistencies, but you and your managers still need to explain the company’s records and operations.

Organized records help diligence move faster and reduce the chance that a buyer interprets missing information as additional risk. You should assign each request to one person for requests, maintain one controlled data room, and continue running the business while the review proceeds. Maintaining performance during exclusivity helps preserve the terms negotiated in the LOI.

Escrow, Indemnification, and Closing

The final purchase agreement determines how much cash you receive at closing and how much remains subject to later adjustment. The closing statement starts with the agreed equity value, then accounts for debt, transaction expenses, estimated working capital, and any escrow holdback. Your M&A advisor should coordinate review of the statement with your attorney and accountant before you authorize the funds flow.

An escrow holdback places part of the purchase price with a third party for a defined period. Buyers may use the escrow to cover valid indemnification claims, such as losses caused by a breach of the seller’s representations in the purchase agreement. A basket establishes the amount of losses that must arise before a buyer can recover, while a cap limits the seller’s exposure for specified claims. The agreement may apply different limits to matters such as taxes, ownership, or fraud.

Most lower-middle-market transactions close about 60 to 120 days after the letter of intent, with the timing determined by the quality of earnings review, working-capital negotiations, financing, and legal documentation. The parties may sign the definitive purchase agreement and close on the same day. Closing may follow several days or weeks later if material conditions remain open.

At closing, the buyer wires funds according to the approved closing statement. You receive the net proceeds after the closing statement deductions. The working-capital true-up usually occurs after closing when final balance-sheet figures become available. From initial preparation through receipt of closing funds, a structured sale commonly takes about six to nine months, although buyer delays or unresolved diligence issues can extend that schedule.

How M&A Advisory Fees Work: Success Fee vs. Retainer Models

M&A advisors typically charge a success fee when a transaction closes. Some advisors also require an upfront retainer or monthly work fee. A success fee may use one percentage for the entire transaction or a Lehman-style formula, which applies declining percentages to successive portions of the transaction value.

The Firmex North American M&A Fee Guide reports benchmark success fees of 6.3% for a $5 million transaction and 2.0% for a $100 million transaction. Its $20 million benchmark is 3.9%. The percentage generally declines as transaction size rises because advisory work does not increase in direct proportion to deal value. A larger transaction can still produce a much higher fee in dollars.

A quoted success fee rarely shows the full cost. The engagement letter may include fixed or recurring charges beyond the success fee. Some advisors credit retainers and work fees against the success fee at closing, while others do not, and the Firmex M&A Fee Guide reports that more advisors have moved toward crediting work fees against the success fee and delaying fee payment when the seller is paid over time. The agreement should also explain how earnouts, rollover equity, assumed debt, and other forms of consideration affect the fee calculation.

Compare the total expected fee at a plausible sale value rather than comparing percentages alone. You should also determine what remains payable if no transaction closes and when fees become due if the buyer pays part of the purchase price later.

A success-fee-only model reduces your upfront cost and limits advisory fees if a sale does not close. An advisor using that model may charge a higher closing fee or require a minimum to compensate for the risk of completing months of unpaid work. A retainer model gives the advisor recurring compensation during the process, which can fit complex assignments that require substantial preparation even if the owner later decides not to sell.

Salt Creek’s standard M&A engagements use a success-fee model with no upfront retainer. Jack and Connor Pitts remain directly involved throughout the transaction. That structure may suit an owner who wants to avoid advisory fees before closing, while another owner may prefer a retainer arrangement based on the assignment's complexity and expected resources.

How to Evaluate Any Sell-Side Advisor, Including Larger Investment Banks

Firm rankings reveal little about how an advisor will handle your transaction. Evaluate each firm against four practical criteria and ask for specific answers before signing an engagement agreement.

Start with the full fee structure. Compare the success fee at a realistic sale price, any minimum fee, and any upfront or monthly charges. Ask whether retainers count toward the closing fee and what you owe if no transaction closes. Salt Creek’s standard engagements use a success fee with no upfront retainer, while other advisors combine a retainer with a closing fee.

Confirm who will perform the work after the engagement begins. Ask which senior banker will attend buyer calls and negotiate key terms. You should also learn who prepares materials and manages diligence. Direct principal access may matter if you want one advisor involved throughout, while a larger team may offer deeper sector coverage or more specialized resources.

Lincoln International illustrates how an institutional model can differ. One independent advisor comparison, not Lincoln's own disclosures, reports that Lincoln's Miami office uses a Lehman-style success fee plus a retainer and places its technology, media, and telecom practice within a $40 million to $500 million enterprise value range. The same source describes institutional firms generally as more likely to delegate execution to vice presidents and analysts. Treat these as one third party's observations about a single office and practice, not Lincoln's firm-wide terms, and confirm the details directly with any firm before you rely on them.

Review how the advisor builds and manages the buyer process. Ask how the firm identifies and qualifies buyers, then sets bid deadlines. A long buyer list provides little benefit if the advisor lacks relevant contacts or approaches buyers without a clear reason they may pursue your company.

Examine confidentiality practices before sharing sensitive information. A careful advisor should explain its disclosure controls, including blind teasers and nondisclosure agreements. Ask who approves each release and how the advisor handles potential competitors.

Choose an advisory model that fits your company’s size and transaction complexity. Consider the senior attention and closing support you expect, along with your budget and confidentiality needs.

Practices That Support a Successful Sale

  • Reconcile financial statements and support adjusted EBITDA before starting outreach. Buyers may lower their offers when early claims do not survive review.
  • Use a blind teaser to protect the company’s identity until a buyer signs a nondisclosure agreement.
  • Check each buyer’s financial capacity and acquisition criteria before making contact so you focus on credible buyers and limit unnecessary disclosure.
  • Test the market before negotiating exclusively with one unsolicited buyer. In Q1 2026, 83% of deals over $5 million attracted at least three offers.
  • Compare the headline price with the offer’s payment timing and closing conditions.
  • Resolve key LOI terms and set a reasonable exclusivity period before signing because exclusivity generally prevents you from seeking competing bids.
  • Plan for your workload during due diligence so you can provide timely, consistent responses.
  • Review the working capital target and all escrow and indemnification terms before closing because they affect how much cash you receive and retain.
  • Evaluate the advisor’s staffing and buyer outreach plan as well as the full fee structure, including charges that apply if no transaction closes.

Conclusion and Next Step

Preparation and process design give you more control over a business sale. Clean financials help buyers assess the company, and a confidential process with multiple qualified buyers provides better information about valuation, deal structure, and buyer fit.
