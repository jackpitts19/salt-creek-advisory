Preparing to Sell July 23, 2026 By Jack Pitts, Co-Founder

When to Start
Exit Planning

Direct Answer

Most owners should begin exit planning three to ten years before they intend to leave. The right window inside that range depends on how you plan to exit and how complex your business is. A third-party sale can move from decision to closing in six to twelve months once the company is prepared, while a family succession often needs five to ten years to develop the next generation and put governance in place, according to an EIX succession planning guide. We have written separately about what that decision feels like from the owner's side in selling a family business in the Midwest.

This guide is written for owners of privately held companies with roughly $2 million to $75 million in revenue who are weighing a sale, an internal succession, or an unsolicited offer. You know your business better than anyone, but you may have never sold one. The earlier you start, the more options you keep open.

Key Takeaways
  • Begin exit planning three to ten years out. A third-party sale can close in six to twelve months once prepared, but family succession often needs five to ten years to develop successors, per Contrail's exit planning guidance.
  • Family transfers fail more often than owners expect. Only about 33% of family businesses reach the second generation, and 13% reach the third, according to an EIX succession planning guide. Written governance and successor development change those odds.
  • Separate leadership succession from ownership succession. You can hand off day-to-day control years before transferring equity, which reduces both tax exposure and family conflict.
  • A retainer does not mean an advisor is more committed. Compare fee models on the fee base and total cost, not the headline percentage.
  • Choose an advisor on fit, not size. Deal-size specialization, senior involvement, and buyer outreach matter more than a firm's name.

Who Needs an Exit Plan and When It Becomes Urgent

Every owner of a privately held company needs an exit plan, because you will leave the business one day whether you choose the timing or something else chooses it for you. The question is not whether to plan but how much runway you give yourself. Urgency escalates as that runway shrinks.

Ten or more years from your intended exit, planning stays informal and low risk. You have time to improve margins, build a management team, and reduce customer concentration before any buyer looks closely. At three to five years, the pressure rises, and you are working against the clock to fix problems that lower your valuation. Inside one year, planning turns critical, and your options narrow to whatever the business already supports, as an EIX succession planning guide notes.

The reason waiting hurts is simple. Value improvements take years to show up in your financials, and buyers pay for demonstrated performance, not promises. A compressed timeline forces you to sell the company as it is rather than as it could be.

Most owners do not start early because a trigger arrives before the plan does. A health crisis, a partner dispute, burnout, or an unsolicited offer can push you toward a decision you have not prepared for. Roughly 20% of owners face an unexpected health event before their planned retirement, according to an EIX succession planning guide. An unsolicited offer can be worth exploring, but one buyer's number rarely reflects what a competitive process would produce. Planning ahead means you respond to these events from a position of readiness rather than reaction.

Exit Planning Timeline: A Phased Checklist

The right runway depends on your exit path. A sale to an outside buyer often closes in under a year once you go to market, so the preparation window compresses. A family transfer or ESOP stretches the timeline because you have to develop successors and build governance before anyone signs anything. The phases below map the three-to-ten-year window down to the closing year. Move faster if you are selling to a third party. Add years if you are transferring to family.

Phase 1: Value Assessment (3 to 10 years out)

  • Get a baseline valuation so you know what your business is worth today, not what you hope it will fetch.
  • Calculate your asset gap. Subtract your retirement needs from your current company value to see how much value you still need to build.
  • Decide which of the six transfer paths fits your goals: family transfer, sale to key employees, an ESOP, a sale to an outside buyer, a merger, or a public offering or liquidation.

Phase 2: Operational and Financial Cleanup (2 to 5 years out)

  • Clean up your financials so a buyer's accountants can verify earnings quickly during due diligence.
  • Reduce customer concentration and build management depth. Buyers pay less for a company that cannot run without you.
  • Update contracts, leases, and any expiring agreements that a buyer will scrutinize.
  • Address the value levers that move your multiple, such as recurring revenue and margin improvement.

Phase 3: Advisor Assembly (1 to 3 years out)

  • Bring in an attorney for legal and contract review, an accountant for tax strategy, and a wealth advisor for your personal plan after the sale.
  • Add an M&A advisor to run the process and manage buyer outreach.
  • Confirm your estate plan is current. About 85% of business owners hold estate plans that are outdated or insufficient.

Phase 4: Market Preparation (6 to 12 months out)

  • Complete a quality of earnings review, an outside check that confirms your reported profit holds up.
  • Build the marketing materials that describe your business to qualified buyers.
  • Identify and research the strategic buyers, private equity firms, and family offices most likely to value what you built.

Phase 5: Transaction Execution (closing year)

  • Run a competitive process so you compare offers rather than negotiating against one buyer.
  • Review letters of intent, the non-binding offers that outline price and structure.
  • Work through due diligence, negotiate the final agreement, and close.

Only 20% to 30% of businesses that go to market actually sell. Starting these phases early is the difference between choosing your terms and accepting whatever the timeline forces on you.

Preparing a Family Business for Succession

Passing a business to family is a separate planning track from selling to an outside buyer, and it fails more often than most owners expect. Only about a third of family businesses reach the second generation, and roughly 13% survive to the third, according to an EIX succession guide. Around 60% of those failures trace back to family conflict, not market conditions or product problems. Those numbers argue for structure well before any handoff.

Start by separating two decisions that owners tend to merge. Leadership succession transfers day-to-day operational control. Ownership succession transfers equity. They can and often should happen at different times. A common approach moves leadership first, then shifts ownership over three to five years through gifting strategies that reduce tax exposure.

Governance structures that prevent conflict

Written agreements do the work that goodwill cannot. A stockholder agreement, operating agreement, or set of bylaws should spell out who appoints and removes officers, which decisions require broad consent, how shares can transfer, and how disputes get resolved, as Katten's advisory guidance outlines. Those provisions matter most when a family member wants early liquidity, leaves the business, or commits a serious breach.

Funded buy-sell agreements handle the liquidity problem directly. Backed by life insurance, a sinking fund, or escrowed proceeds, they let a departing shareholder receive fair value without forcing a distressed sale. Underfunded buy-sell provisions and vague trust language are the red flags that Inspire's guide on family business conflict resolution flags most often.

A family council and a written governance charter keep family decisions separate from business management. Regular meetings with set agendas give relatives a forum that does not spill into the operating company.

Preparing a successor, or accepting that none is ready

Successor development is deliberate, not automatic. An honest competency assessment, cross-functional rotations, outside work experience, and authority that expands against measurable milestones together span roughly five to ten years.

Sometimes no family member is willing or ready to lead, and that is a legitimate outcome rather than a failure. You still have real paths. You can bring in professional non-family management while your family retains ownership, structure an ESOP, or sell to a strategic or financial buyer. A well-run sale can protect employees, culture, and legacy as effectively as an internal transfer, and for many families it produces a cleaner result.

Common Mistakes That Derail an Exit

Most failed exits trace back to a handful of preventable decisions made too early or ignored too long. Each mistake below narrows your options at the moment you most need them open.

Waiting too long to start. When exit prep begins under a year before you want to leave, you lose the ability to fix operational or financial problems that depress value. Only 20 to 30% of businesses that go to market actually sell, according to IEPA, and compressed timelines are a common reason deals fall apart.

Leaving the plan undocumented. 43% of family-owned businesses operate without a formal succession plan, per research summarized by EIX. When a health crisis or partner dispute forces a sudden transition, an undocumented plan leaves your family and management guessing at your intentions.

Failing to prepare the successor. 25% of succession attempts fail because the heir was unprepared. Handing authority to someone who has not been objectively assessed or given real operating experience often ends in a distressed sale a few years later.

Ignoring tax and legal structure. Estate taxes can reach 40%, and 85% of owners hold estate plans that are outdated or insufficient. Skipping this work can hand a large share of your proceeds to the government instead of your family.

Assuming one buyer's offer reflects full value. An unsolicited offer can be worth exploring, but accepting it without a competitive process means you never learn what other buyers would pay. Fixing these mistakes is where a qualified advisor earns their fee.

How M&A Advisors Charge: Comparing Fee Models

Most M&A advisor fees fall into three broad shapes. A retainer-plus-success-fee model charges a monthly or upfront work fee, usually credited back against a larger fee paid only when the deal closes. A Lehman or tiered structure sets the success fee as a sliding percentage across bands of deal value, either declining as the deal grows or accelerating to reward a higher price. A flat percentage applies one rate to the whole transaction.

The retainer confirms an owner is serious and offsets the advisor's upfront work, since preparation, buyer research, and diligence support begin long before any check clears. For private companies, retainers commonly run between $50,000 and $100,000, often paid monthly and credited at closing, according to Founders Advisors. Whether that retainer signals commitment or simply shifts cost forward depends on the engagement letter, not the label.

The table below compares each model on how it is structured, its typical range, and the owner it tends to fit. Read the tradeoffs before the percentages, because the incentive built into a fee shapes how hard an advisor pushes on price.

Fee Model Comparison

Fee Model How It Is Structured Typical Range Best Fit
Retainer + success fee Monthly or upfront work fee, credited against a success fee at close $50K–$100K retainer plus 1%–6% of deal value Owners who want a full process and accept upfront cost credited later
Lehman / modified Lehman Declining percentage across value bands (5-4-3-2-1 or flattened variants) Roughly 2%–5.5% blended on a $10M deal Larger deals where the advisor's effort is spread across more value
Flat percentage One rate applied to the whole transaction 1%–6%, often with a minimum fee Smaller deals wanting a simple, predictable calculation
Tiered / accelerator Rising percentage on higher value bands Varies by breakpoints Owners who want the advisor's incentive tied to a higher final price
Hourly / hybrid Time-based billing, sometimes combined with a smaller success fee $200–$900/hr depending on seniority Narrow, defined work rather than a full sale process
Success fee, no upfront retainer No monthly or upfront fee, paid only at close Negotiated percentage of deal value Owners who prefer the advisor's pay tied entirely to a completed transaction

We use the success-fee model with no upfront retainer for standard M&A engagements, so our fee is paid at closing rather than during the process. Boutique and independent advisors sometimes carry higher success-fee percentages in exchange for skipping the retainer, according to CT Acquisitions, so the headline rate deserves scrutiny under any model.

The percentage matters less than the fee base it applies to. Two proposals quoting the same rate can produce very different total fees depending on whether the fee is calculated on enterprise value, equity value, or aggregate consideration, and how debt, cash, working capital, earnouts, and rollover equity are counted. Enterprise value describes what the business itself is worth, while equity value is what reaches the owner after debt. Before signing, model the total fee across several realistic outcomes and clarify in writing whether contingent payments like earnouts are included, since that definition can shift your real cost by one to two percentage points.

How to Choose an M&A Advisor

The right advisor depends on your company, not on which firm has the biggest name. An advisor who handles $200 million transactions may treat a $12 million sale as a minor account, while a Main Street broker may lack the buyer reach to run a competitive process for a company with $3 million of adjusted EBITDA. Start by confirming that a firm regularly works in your size range and industry, because deal-size specialization shapes how much attention and how many qualified buyers you actually get. Our comparison of M&A advisors for a $20 million sale shows what that looks like at one size, and our survey of the top lower middle market investment banks covers the wider field.

Ask who does the work after the engagement letter is signed. Many firms sell the deal with a senior partner and then hand the process to junior staff who manage the negotiation, the buyer calls, and the diligence. That matters because the person negotiating your price and reading buyer signals should be the person with the most experience. A founder-led firm keeps that judgment in the room the whole way through, which is one reason some owners prefer it. It is one legitimate structure among several, and larger firms with deep bench staffing can be the better fit for complex, multi-jurisdiction deals.

Examine the buyer outreach process before you sign. A serious sell-side process reaches strategic buyers, private equity firms, family offices, and independent sponsors through a targeted, researched list, not a public listing. Ask how the firm builds that list, how many buyers it typically contacts, and how it screens them, because a wider, better-qualified pool is what creates real competition on price and terms.

Look at how fees line up with your outcome. A success-fee model ties most of the advisor's pay to the closing, which keeps the firm's incentive close to yours, though a retainer signals commitment and covers real process costs for some firms. We use a success-fee model with no upfront retainer for standard M&A engagements, which removes the cost of starting for owners who are still deciding whether to sell. Neither model is automatically better. Model the total fee across realistic outcomes and read how the fee base is defined.

Finally, ask how the firm protects confidentiality. Employees, customers, and competitors should not learn you are exploring a sale from a leaked process. A disciplined advisor uses non-disclosure agreements, staged information release, and blind teasers to control who knows what and when. Weigh all of these against your priorities, because the best advisor is the one whose strengths match the decision in front of you.

Practical Example

Consider a hypothetical company with $3 million in adjusted EBITDA. Adjusted EBITDA describes the company's earnings after adding back owner-specific and one-time expenses. The owner is 61, has no family successor ready to lead, and receives an unsolicited offer from a strategic buyer. She has roughly two years before she wants to step back, which is enough time to clean up financials and run a competitive process rather than accept the single offer.

At a $15 million sale price, the fee model changes what she keeps. A traditional retainer-plus-success-fee arrangement might cost $75,000 in retainers before closing, credited against a success fee. A success-fee-only model charges nothing upfront, so her out-of-pocket cost stays at zero until the deal closes. If both structures land near the same percentage at closing, the difference lands in cash flow and risk during the process. She carries no fee if the deal never closes.

The right structure depends on her tolerance for upfront cost and how confident she is in the outcome.

Conclusion and Next Step

Timing and advisor fit are decisions you make deliberately, not defaults you accept by waiting. The owners who do best treat exit planning as work that starts years before any transaction, and they choose an advisor based on senior involvement, buyer process, and fee alignment rather than firm size alone.
