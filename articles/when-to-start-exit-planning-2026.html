Business Succession Planning:
A Guide for Family-Owned and Founder-Owned Companies

Starting succession planning several years before an internal handoff or transaction gives you more time to prepare a successor, improve transferable value, and preserve alternative exit paths.

Business succession planning gives family-owned and founder-owned companies time to decide who will lead, who will own the business, and whether an eventual internal transition or sale will best serve the owner's goals. Effective succession planning for business owners also improves valuation readiness, preserves strategic options, and prepares the company to create buyer competition if business exit planning ultimately leads to a transaction.

Key Takeaways
  • Begin business succession planning several years before the intended transition when possible. Contrail's exit planning guidance recommends allowing five to ten years for a family succession, while a prepared third-party sale may close within six to twelve months.
  • Family businesses often struggle to complete multigenerational transitions. According to the EIX succession planning guide, about 33% reach the second generation and 13% reach the third. Written governance and successor development can address common causes of failed transitions, including family conflict and an unprepared successor.
  • Separate leadership succession from ownership succession. You can hand off day-to-day control years before transferring equity, which can make the transition easier to manage and give the successor time to demonstrate readiness before receiving equity.
  • Evaluate a retainer based on the work it covers, whether it is credited at closing, and how it affects the total fee. Compare fee models on the fee base and total cost, not the headline percentage.
  • Involve an M&A advisor before a transaction becomes immediate. Early input can connect succession choices to valuation readiness, buyer expectations, and the competitive process you may eventually run.
  • Choose an advisor on fit, not size. Deal-size specialization, senior involvement, and buyer outreach matter more than a firm's name.

Business Succession Planning Definitions

Business succession planning is the process of deciding how leadership and ownership will transfer over time. It prepares the company for an internal handoff, family transition, or eventual sale before any single outcome becomes urgent.

Business exit planning is preparation for executing a specific owner exit, such as a third-party sale, family transfer, employee sale, merger, or liquidation. It overlaps with succession planning but becomes transaction-focused once the owner chooses a path and timing.

Ownership succession is the transfer of company equity and its economic rights. It may occur before, during, or after a leadership change.

Leadership succession is the transfer of operating authority and responsibility for running the company. The next leader may be a family member, an employee, or an outside executive and does not necessarily need to own the business.

The two processes should inform each other. A family transition still needs valuation, governance, and financial preparation, while an eventual third-party sale benefits from years spent building management depth and reducing risks that buyers will discount.

How Business Owners Start Succession Planning and When It Becomes Urgent

Every privately held company will eventually face a change in ownership, leadership, or both. A succession plan lets you prepare for that change before a planned transition or unexpected event forces a decision. Start by documenting your desired role and timing, possible future leaders and owners, and acceptable alternatives if the preferred successor is unavailable. Assess whether the company can operate without you and whether its financial records and governance documents are ready for a transfer. Compare a baseline valuation with your personal financial needs. A shorter planning period limits the changes you can make before the transition.

Ten or more years before your intended transition, you can explore succession paths and begin long-term operational improvements. You have time to build a record of stronger margins and reduce the company's dependence on you or a small number of customers before a buyer begins due diligence. At three to five years, focus on changes that need a documented performance history, including stronger earnings and lower dependence on individual customers or the owner. Inside one year, you can still prepare for a transition, but changes that need several reporting periods may not affect a buyer's valuation. The EIX succession planning guide provides additional guidance on planning timelines.

Buyers generally assess demonstrated financial performance, so improvements may need several reporting periods to influence valuation. With a compressed timeline, recent operational changes may not appear in the historical results buyers use to support their valuation.

An unexpected event can force an owner to make succession decisions before a plan is ready. A health crisis, a partner dispute, burnout, or an unsolicited offer can push you toward a decision you have not prepared for. The EIX succession planning guide reports that roughly 20% of owners face an unexpected health event before their planned retirement. An unsolicited offer can be worth exploring, but one buyer's number rarely reflects what a competitive process would produce. A documented succession plan gives you preselected leadership, ownership, and sale options when an unexpected event occurs.

Business Succession and Exit Planning Timeline: A Phased Checklist

Your succession path determines the timeline. A prepared third-party sale may close within a year after going to market, but exit planning should begin earlier. A family transfer or employee stock ownership plan may require additional time to prepare the successor and establish the financing and governance structure. The phases below cover the period between initial planning and the closing or handoff year.

Phase 1: Succession Goals and Value Assessment (3 to 10 years out)

  • Define when you want to step back, what role you want afterward, and whether leadership and ownership will transfer together or separately.
  • Identify potential family, employee, or outside successors and set a contingency path if no internal candidate becomes ready.
  • Get a baseline valuation so you know what the business may be worth under current conditions.
  • Estimate your funding gap. Compare the after-tax proceeds you may receive from the company, together with your other available assets, against the amount needed to support your personal financial plan.
  • Compare the transfer paths that may fit your goals, including a family transfer, a sale to key employees, an ESOP, a sale to an outside buyer, a merger, a public offering, or liquidation.
  • Speak with an M&A advisor early enough to understand how buyers may view the company, which value gaps need time to address, and what preparation would support a competitive process if a sale becomes the preferred path.

Phase 2: Operational and Financial Cleanup (2 to 5 years out)

  • Prepare accurate, consistent financial statements and supporting records so a buyer's accountants can verify earnings during due diligence.
  • Reduce customer concentration and build management depth. Buyers pay less for a company that cannot run without you.
  • Update contracts, leases, and any expiring agreements that a buyer will scrutinize.
  • Strengthen the operating characteristics buyers may reflect in their valuation, such as recurring revenue or sustained margin improvement.

Phase 3: Advisor Assembly (1 to 3 years out)

  • Bring in an attorney for legal and contract review, an accountant for tax strategy, and a wealth advisor for your personal plan after the sale.
  • If you select a sale, formally engage an M&A advisor to prepare the company, develop the buyer strategy, conduct outreach, and manage the process. Early readiness work can give the advisor a head start on transaction preparation.
  • Confirm that your estate plan is current. The Institute for Exit Planning reports that 85% of business owners have estate plans it considers outdated or insufficient.

Phase 4: Market Preparation (6 to 12 months out)

  • Consider a sell-side quality of earnings review, in which an independent accounting firm analyzes the components and sustainability of reported earnings.
  • Build the marketing materials that describe your business to qualified buyers.
  • Identify and research the strategic buyers, private equity firms, and family offices most likely to value what you built.

Phase 5: Transaction or Transition Execution (closing year)

  • For a third-party sale, run a competitive process so you can compare offers rather than negotiate with only one buyer.
  • For an internal transition, confirm leadership authority, ownership-transfer terms, governance responsibilities, and the outgoing owner's role.
  • Review letters of intent, the non-binding offers that outline price and structure.
  • Work through due diligence, negotiate the final agreement, and close.

The Institute for Exit Planning estimates that 20% to 30% of businesses taken to market ultimately sell. Starting early gives you more time to correct issues that could prevent a transaction or weaken its terms.

Preparing a Family Business for Succession or an Eventual Sale

A family transfer requires a different execution process than a third-party sale, but both paths benefit from early preparation. According to the EIX succession guide, about one-third of family businesses reach the second generation and roughly 13% reach the third. Unclear authority, compensation, and ownership expectations can create conflict during a transition.

Prepare for the preferred family transition and a credible alternative. Document governance, develop potential leaders, strengthen financial reporting, reduce dependence on the current owner, and obtain a baseline valuation. That preparation supports an internal transfer while preserving the option to pursue a competitive sale if no successor is ready.

Start by separating two decisions that owners tend to merge. Leadership succession transfers day-to-day operational control. Ownership succession transfers equity. They can and often should happen at different times. One approach transfers leadership first and shifts ownership later through a structure reviewed by the owner's tax and legal advisors.

Governance structures that prevent conflict

Written agreements clarify authority, transfer rights, and dispute procedures before a conflict arises. A stockholder agreement, operating agreement, or set of bylaws should spell out who appoints and removes officers, which decisions require broad consent, how shares can transfer, and how disputes are resolved, as Katten's family-business succession guidance outlines. Those provisions matter most when a family member wants early liquidity, leaves the business, or commits a serious breach.

Funded buy-sell agreements handle the liquidity problem directly. Backed by life insurance, a sinking fund, or escrowed proceeds, they let a departing shareholder receive fair value without forcing a distressed sale. Inspire's family-business conflict guidance identifies underfunded buy-sell provisions and vague trust language as potential sources of conflict.

A family council and a written governance charter keep family decisions separate from business management. Regular meetings with set agendas give relatives a forum that does not spill into the operating company.

Preparing a successor, or accepting that none is ready

A successor needs planned experience and progressively greater responsibility before taking control. A development plan can combine a competency assessment, experience across business functions, outside work experience, and increasing authority tied to measurable milestones. The timeline should reflect the candidate's experience and the complexity of the role.

If no family member is willing or ready to lead, you can evaluate outside management, an employee ownership structure, or a third-party sale. You can bring in professional non-family management while your family retains ownership, structure an ESOP, or sell to a strategic or financial buyer. If a sale becomes the preferred path, prepare for buyer diligence and competitive outreach rather than relying on a single unsolicited offer. Sale terms can address employee continuity, culture, and legacy alongside price, although the outcome will depend on the buyer and negotiated protections.

Common Mistakes That Derail Business Succession

Address the following issues early enough to preserve your succession options and document any operational or financial improvement before a transfer.

Waiting too long to start. When exit preparation begins less than a year before you want to leave, you may not have enough reporting periods to demonstrate sustained improvements in earnings, customer concentration, or management depth. Starting earlier gives buyers evidence that operational changes have produced durable results.

Leaving the plan undocumented. According to research summarized by EIX, 43% of family-owned businesses operate without a formal succession plan. When a health crisis or partner dispute forces a sudden transition, an undocumented plan leaves your family and management guessing at your intentions.

Failing to prepare the successor. An unprepared successor may struggle to assume full operating authority. Assess candidates against the role's requirements and give them meaningful operating responsibility before the handoff.

Ignoring tax and legal structure. Federal transfer-tax rates and exemptions may affect some ownership transfers, while outdated estate documents can create conflicts with the succession plan. Ask tax and legal advisors to model the consequences of the proposed structure and update the relevant documents.

Assuming one buyer's offer reflects full value. An unsolicited offer can be worth exploring, but accepting it without a competitive process means you never learn what other buyers would pay. An M&A advisor can help evaluate an unsolicited offer, identify other potential buyers, and compare price with non-price terms.

How M&A Advisors Charge: Comparing Fee Models

M&A advisors commonly use retainers, success fees, tiered formulas, flat percentages, or hybrid arrangements. A retainer-plus-success-fee model charges a monthly or upfront work fee that may be credited against the success fee due at closing. A Lehman-style formula applies specified percentages to successive bands of transaction value. An accelerator instead applies a higher fee rate above an agreed threshold. A flat percentage applies one rate to the whole transaction.

A retainer compensates the advisor for preparation, buyer research, and diligence support performed before closing. Some advisors also use it to confirm that the owner is committed to the process. For private-company engagements, Founders Advisors reports retainers of $50,000 to $100,000, often paid monthly and credited against the success fee at closing. Whether that retainer signals commitment or simply shifts cost forward depends on the engagement letter, not the label.

Compare each model by its fee base, total cost at several sale prices, payment timing, minimum fee, and treatment of contingent consideration. Those terms reveal more than the headline percentage about the advisor's compensation.

Salt Creek Advisory uses a success-fee model with no upfront retainer for standard M&A engagements, so clients pay our fee at closing rather than during the process. CT Acquisitions notes that boutique and independent advisors may charge higher success-fee percentages when they do not require a retainer. Compare the total fee under realistic sale outcomes rather than relying on the quoted percentage.

The fee base used to calculate an M&A advisor's compensation can matter as much as the quoted percentage. Two proposals quoting the same rate can produce different total fees. The calculation may use enterprise value, equity value, or aggregate consideration, and the engagement letter may treat debt, cash, working capital, earnouts, and rollover equity differently. Enterprise value generally reflects the value of the company's operations before adjustments for debt and cash. Equity value reflects the value attributable to shareholders after the transaction's agreed debt, cash, and other adjustments. Before signing, model the total fee across several realistic outcomes and clarify in writing whether contingent payments like earnouts are included, because that definition can materially change the total fee.

How to Choose an M&A Advisor

Choose an advisor whose typical transaction size, industry experience, staffing model, and buyer reach fit your company. An advisor who handles $200 million transactions may treat a $12 million sale as a minor account, while a Main Street broker may lack the buyer reach to run a competitive process for a company with $3 million of adjusted EBITDA. Start by confirming that a firm regularly works in your size range and industry, because deal-size specialization shapes how much attention and how many qualified buyers you actually get. Our comparison of M&A advisors for a $20 million sale examines firms serving that transaction range. Our survey of lower-middle-market investment banks covers a broader set of providers.

Ask who will handle the engagement after you sign. Confirm whether the senior professionals who lead the pitch will also manage negotiations, buyer calls, and due diligence after you sign. Experienced senior involvement can be particularly important during negotiations and when interpreting buyer feedback. A founder-led firm may provide more direct senior involvement, but confirm the staffing model before signing. At Salt Creek Advisory, Jack and Connor Pitts work directly with owners during negotiations, buyer calls, and due diligence. Larger firms may offer broader staffing and specialist resources for complex or multi-jurisdictional transactions.

Examine the buyer outreach process before you sign. A targeted sell-side process identifies qualified strategic and financial buyers through research rather than relying only on a public listing. Ask how the firm builds and screens its buyer list. Outreach to multiple qualified buyers can create competition on price and terms, although the appropriate number varies by company and market.

Compare when each fee becomes payable and how the amount changes with the transaction value. A success fee makes most compensation contingent on closing, while a retainer pays for work performed before a transaction closes and may remain payable if no sale occurs. We use a success-fee model with no upfront retainer for standard M&A engagements, which removes the cost of starting for owners who are still deciding whether to sell. Compare both models by calculating the total fee across realistic sale outcomes and reviewing the definition of the fee base.

Ask how the firm protects confidentiality throughout buyer outreach and due diligence. A controlled process limits disclosure to employees, customers, and competitors until sharing the information becomes necessary. An advisor can use non-disclosure agreements and staged information release to limit what prospective buyers learn at each point in the process. Evaluate confidentiality controls alongside deal-size experience, senior involvement, buyer outreach, and total fees.

Practical Example

Consider a hypothetical company with $3 million in adjusted EBITDA. Adjusted EBITDA describes the company's earnings after adding back owner-specific and one-time expenses. The owner is 61, has completed an initial succession review but has no family successor ready to lead, and receives an unsolicited offer from a strategic buyer. She has roughly two years before she wants to step back. That period may allow her to improve financial reporting and run a competitive process, although the achievable preparation will depend on the company's condition.

If the company sells for $15 million, the two proposals may differ in payment timing and total cost. For illustration, one retainer-plus-success-fee proposal might require $75,000 in retainers before closing and credit those payments against the success fee. A success-fee-only model charges nothing upfront, so her out-of-pocket cost stays at zero until the deal closes. If both structures produce a similar total fee at closing, their main difference is payment timing. Under the hypothetical success-fee-only proposal, she pays no advisory fee if the deal does not close, subject to any expense-reimbursement or termination provisions in the engagement letter. Under a retainer model, whether she recovers or receives credit for prior payments depends on the engagement letter.

She should compare the total fee, upfront payment, minimum fee, termination terms, and services included under each proposal.

Starting succession planning several years before an internal handoff or transaction gives you more time to prepare a successor, improve transferable value, and preserve alternative exit paths. Early planning connects leadership and ownership decisions with valuation readiness and preserves the option to create buyer competition.
