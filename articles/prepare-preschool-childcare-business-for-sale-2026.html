Preparing to Sell September 17, 2026 By Jack Pitts, Co-Founder

How to Prepare a
Preschool or Childcare Business for Sale in 2026

Preschool and childcare owners should begin 12 to 24 months before going to market. Buyers test classroom-level enrollment against usable capacity, staffing ratios, credential files, licensing history, and lease rights. The final three to six months assemble the records and sequence the approvals that decide when a sale can actually close.

TL;DR
  • Use the 12 to 24 months before a sale to fix structural issues involving enrollment records, staffing stability, owner dependence, accounting, licensing files, leases, and facility condition.
  • Buyers verify classroom-level enrollment against usable capacity, attendance, billing, collections, staff credentials, and required ratios. Headline occupancy and waitlist totals rarely answer those questions.
  • Use the final 3 to 6 months to assemble diligence records, support adjusted EBITDA, confirm insurance coverage, and sequence closing around regulatory approvals.
  • Licensing, subsidy participation, food programs, and change-of-ownership procedures may require separate approvals. Requirements vary by jurisdiction, so confirm each process with the applicable state and local agencies.
  • The guide focuses on childcare-specific sale readiness. Separate Salt Creek resources address valuation multiples and advisor selection.

Why childcare sale preparation is different from a typical small business sale

Childcare buyers connect each classroom’s revenue to the staff, space, and regulatory approvals needed to serve enrolled children. A headline occupancy rate can conceal an unusable classroom, an unstaffed infant room, or a waitlist that cannot convert into paid enrollment.

Payer mix and collection timing affect working capital and the cash flow a buyer can expect after closing. Families pay about 68% of childcare costs in the United States, with public funding and other sources covering the balance. Buyers examine private-pay and subsidy revenue separately because reimbursement timing, eligibility rules, and deferred tuition can affect working capital and cash flow.

Staffing limits how much enrollment can produce. Personnel costs commonly account for 50% to 60% of a childcare provider’s expenses, and state child-to-staff ratios often require more labor in infant and toddler rooms. A buyer therefore tests enrollment and payroll by classroom rather than assuming additional children will generate the same margin across the center.

Licensing and facility risks receive similar scrutiny because either can reduce usable capacity or delay closing. Buyers review inspection and incident records, staff credentials, lease rights, and change-of-ownership requirements. Specific licensing procedures vary by jurisdiction and require confirmation with the relevant agencies.

12 to 24 months out: building a business that supports buyer diligence

Use the 12 to 24 months before a sale to correct structural weaknesses and establish an operating history that buyers can verify. Consistent financial records, compliance files, and operating results carry more weight than documents assembled shortly before marketing. Unresolved problems may lower valuation, extend due diligence, or lead buyers to seek additional contractual protection.

Reconcile enrollment, waitlists, and capacity by classroom

A waitlist does not prove that a center can add children. Staffing ratios, classroom availability, and age-group demand determine whether listed families can become enrolled families. In a 2024 NAEYC survey, 56% of directors reported under-enrollment relative to available slots, primarily because of staffing shortages, compensation, and affordability constraints, as reported in the AEFP Live Handbook.

Buyers calculate occupancy by classroom rather than accepting one center-wide percentage. They start with licensed capacity by room and age group, then reduce that ceiling for closed rooms, staffing limits, facility restrictions, and schedule conflicts to determine usable capacity. A full-time-equivalent count converts part-time schedules into comparable occupied slots, while paid enrollment distinguishes billed children from children who merely appear on attendance records.

Your waitlist should connect demand to specific openings. The log should record each child’s age, requested schedule, desired start date, and latest family contact. It should also show any placement offered and the final outcome. Classroom-level conversion and withdrawal records help buyers distinguish active demand from duplicate, outdated, or unsuitable inquiries.

Monthly reconciliation creates the support buyers expect during diligence. Match classroom rosters to attendance records, and match tuition billed to cash collected. Document timing differences, discounts, unpaid balances, and children whose attendance does not match the billing system. These matched records allow buyers to test reported enrollment and revenue against claims about the center’s capacity.

Consistent records help support the revenue and earnings used in an adjusted EBITDA calculation. If enrollment reports cannot support recorded tuition revenue, buyers may reduce recurring revenue and earnings during diligence. Building classroom-level reports 12 to 24 months before a sale gives you a credible history instead of a last-minute snapshot.

Prepare tuition, subsidy, and deferred revenue records for financial diligence

Buyers test private-pay and subsidy revenue separately because billing terms, collection timing, and supporting records differ. Your monthly revenue schedules should identify payer type, classroom, service period, billed amount, and cash received. Consistent receivable aging and a documented write-off policy help buyers distinguish collectible balances from overstated revenue.

A monthly reconciliation of four records gives buyers direct support for reported revenue. For each classroom, match enrollment rosters and attendance records with billing records and cash receipts. Investigate differences caused by discounts, refunds, denied subsidy days, unpaid family balances, or revenue recorded in the wrong month. Buyers often repeat this reconciliation during financial diligence, so resolving discrepancies early can reduce later adjustments to earnings.

Prepaid tuition requires separate tracking because cash collection does not always equal earned revenue. When a family pays before you provide care, the payment is generally recorded as deferred revenue or another contract liability until the center earns it. The precise accounting treatment depends on the enrollment agreement and refund terms, as described in PwC’s guidance on contract liabilities. During a sale, buyers may include deferred revenue in normal working capital or negotiate another treatment, including classification as a debt-like item. Buyers assess the appropriate treatment using historical accounting and the remaining cost of providing prepaid services.

CACFP receivables also need their own reconciliation. Match eligible attendance and meals served with claims filed, reimbursements received, and denied or adjusted claims. Program rules vary by jurisdiction. For example, California requires a separate CACFP transfer process when a qualifying ownership change occurs.

Start a normalized EBITDA schedule during this preparation window. Normalized EBITDA adjusts earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization for documented owner compensation differences, personal expenses, and unusual costs or income. Building the schedule early gives you time to preserve invoices, contracts, payroll support, and explanations before a buyer’s quality-of-earnings review tests each adjustment. If supporting records are missing, buyers may reject an add-back or reduce its amount.

Stabilize directors and teachers, and reduce owner dependence

Your center’s actual staffing history will carry more weight than an industry turnover average. Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland research found that roughly one-third of surveyed centers had annual turnover of 20% or more, while 44% reported no turnover. Because results varied widely among respondents, buyers will learn more from your center’s staffing history than from an industry average.

Build a monthly record showing each employee’s role, classroom, hire date, termination date, credentials, and compensation. Buyers use these records to measure director and teacher tenure and to confirm whether staffing supports current enrollment. Frequent departures can raise recruiting costs and disrupt classroom capacity, so buyers may reflect unstable staffing in a lower valuation or more protective deal terms.

Documented management depth makes the business easier to transfer. Your director should be able to manage daily staffing and parent communication without routinely relying on you. Written job descriptions, clear reporting responsibilities, and backup coverage show buyers that one departure will not interrupt operations. When a long-tenured director holds most operational knowledge, a buyer may request a retention plan for that director. If the owner still performs essential duties, the buyer may instead request a defined post-closing transition period.

Owner dependence also affects adjusted EBITDA. If you regularly cover classrooms, manage enrollment, or perform director duties, a buyer will estimate the cost of replacing that work. Adding back your full compensation without deducting a market-rate replacement salary can overstate the cash flow available to a buyer.

Use the 12- to 24-month preparation window to transfer recurring responsibilities and test whether managers can handle them. Track who approves schedules and resolves staff issues. Buyers can then verify that the business operates through its management structure rather than through the departing owner.

Put staff credential files, background checks, and training records in order

Prepare a complete compliance file for each employee and contractor whose work affects classroom ratios or licensed capacity. Build a current staff roster that links each person to required clearances, credentials, training records, renewal dates, and unresolved exceptions. Reconcile names across payroll, classroom schedules, and personnel files so buyers can confirm who may legally work in each role.

Federal Child Care and Development Fund rules establish a baseline for comprehensive background checks, but states administer those checks and may add requirements. Federal rules generally require covered childcare staff and prospective staff to undergo background checks before employment and at least once every five years, subject to provisions governing supervised provisional employment. The Caring for Our Children Basics guidance summarizes these requirements. Required searches can include fingerprint-based criminal history, sex-offender registries, and child-abuse or neglect registries. Prior residence in another state may trigger additional searches.

Credential files should document that each director and teacher meets the requirements for the assigned position. Depending on local rules, buyers may request degrees, transcripts, teaching credentials, director approvals, orientation records, CPR and first-aid certifications, safe-sleep training, and continuing education. Confirm the exact requirements and renewal cycles with the applicable state agency.

Missing or expired records can reduce usable capacity even when the facility retains unused licensed seats. If a worker cannot count toward required staffing ratios, a buyer may treat the affected classroom as unavailable until the issue is resolved. Completing this work 12 to 24 months before a sale gives you time to renew checks, recover missing records, and hire qualified replacements when necessary.

Clean up licensing standing, inspections, and incident history

A complete regulatory file gives buyers a clear basis for evaluating licensing risk before they plan a change of ownership. Buyers can compare your disclosures with state inspection reports, complaint records, and other agency files. Missing records or inconsistent explanations can extend diligence even when the underlying issue was minor.

Organize every inspection report, complaint notice, incident log, regulator letter, and corrective-action plan by location and date. For each violation or reported incident, include evidence of remediation and any written confirmation that the agency closed the matter. Disclose open investigations clearly rather than presenting an incomplete history.

Unresolved violations can change deal terms as well as the closing schedule. A buyer may require remediation before closing or seek protection through indemnities and escrow. Repeated ratio violations or incomplete incident reporting may also raise questions about management practices and the reliability of licensed capacity.

ChildCare.gov explains that states and territories set childcare licensing requirements, including health and safety standards, staff qualifications, and ongoing monitoring. Inspection schedules, classroom ratios, and group-size limits vary by jurisdiction and may change usable capacity or staffing costs. Confirm current requirements and change-of-ownership procedures directly with the applicable licensing agency before setting a transaction timeline. This guide provides transaction-planning information rather than legal advice, so use qualified counsel to interpret the rules for your jurisdiction and transaction.

Address the lease, owned real estate, and facility condition

Review real estate early because landlord approvals, property repairs, and use restrictions can take months to resolve. A buyer may decline a location if continued occupancy remains uncertain, while lenders may require major facility issues to be addressed before closing.

For leased sites, review every lease, amendment, renewal option, and landlord communication. Confirm the remaining term, assignment requirements, change-of-control language, consent standard, review fees, and any landlord recapture right. A recapture clause may let the landlord terminate the lease after an assignment request, which can threaten the transaction when the location supports much of the business’s value. Commercial lease terms vary by contract and state, so counsel should interpret the specific provisions.

You should also collect certificates of occupancy, zoning approvals, and conditional-use permits. Preserve documentation showing that the property permits childcare for the current capacity and age groups. Local zoning and state licensing operate as separate layers, and a buyer should confirm both with the applicable authorities. For example, District of Columbia guidance applies childcare-specific restrictions to some residential properties.

Owned property requires separate analysis of the operating business and the real estate. If the business pays no rent or pays a related-party amount, normalize adjusted EBITDA using supportable market rent. A contemplated sale-leaseback also needs a lease with adequate term, renewal rights, predictable rent, and continued childcare use.

Identify and document deferred maintenance before marketing the business. A commercial property condition assessment following ASTM E2018 guidance can identify observed deficiencies and estimate near-term capital needs for major building systems. Review playground inspection reports, repair logs, surfacing records, and open corrective items against the CPSC playground safety guidance.

Unresolved lease rights can reduce the buyer pool or stall closing. Deferred maintenance may lower the achievable price, shift repair costs to you, or produce an escrow holdback. Addressing both categories early gives you time to negotiate, repair, or document the issue before a buyer controls the timetable.

Final 3 to 6 months: converting readiness into a closable transaction

Use the final three to six months to assemble the diligence data room and document adjusted EBITDA before buyer review begins. At the same time, verify insurance coverage and establish a closing schedule based on jurisdiction-specific change-of-ownership requirements. Completing these tasks before diligence reduces avoidable record requests and exposes regulatory timing issues before they disrupt negotiations.

Assemble the diligence data room buyers will actually test

A buyer will test whether your financial records support the earnings and cash flows presented in the sale materials. Build the data room around monthly financial statements, tax returns, bank statements, payroll records, debt schedules, and general ledger detail. Add childcare-specific schedules for enrollment and billing by classroom, subsidy receivables, prepaid tuition, deferred revenue, and food-program reimbursements.

Prepare a quality-of-earnings file that reconciles reported results to adjusted EBITDA, including supportable adjustments for nonrecurring or owner-specific expenses. Because buyers often apply a valuation multiple to EBITDA, financial diligence focuses heavily on whether those earnings will continue after closing, as explained in Grant Thornton’s financial due diligence guidance. Each adjustment should include the amount, accounting period, explanation, invoice or payroll support, and any replacement cost.

Cash-basis records can affect price and deal terms because buyers may need an accrual-based analysis that matches revenue and expenses to the periods in which care was provided. As Kreischer Miller’s revenue diligence guidance explains, buyers may require additional work to analyze cash-basis financials. Unresolved timing differences can then delay diligence or affect the earnings they accept. For a childcare business, the conversion may change the timing of tuition revenue, subsidy receivables, payroll liabilities, and prepaid tuition.

Working capital and net debt schedules also need transaction-ready support. When the deal structure includes working capital, buyers may set a target for the normal level of operating current assets and liabilities delivered at closing. Missing receivables, unrecorded liabilities, or inconsistent deferred revenue treatment can produce a closing adjustment. When the purchase price is calculated on a cash-free, debt-free basis, buyers subtract agreed debt and debt-like obligations to determine equity proceeds. Undocumented loans, unpaid taxes, or other liabilities may then reduce proceeds or prompt an escrow request.

A reconciled file allows buyers to test reported EBITDA without first rebuilding the underlying records, which can reduce follow-up requests and disputes over proposed adjustments.

Confirm change-of-ownership, subsidy, and program-approval procedures by state

Licensing approval often sets the practical closing date because a signed purchase agreement may not authorize the buyer to operate the center. You should map each required permit, subsidy enrollment, and program approval before setting the transaction schedule. Buyers may require these approvals as closing conditions or hold back part of the purchase price until they are complete.

Kentucky illustrates the licensing risk. A childcare center license cannot be sold or transferred, and the incoming owner must obtain approval before assuming ownership. The state can treat a center that changes ownership without approval as operating illegally, according to the Kentucky Division of Regulated Child Care.

Georgia treats related approvals as separate workstreams. The state recognizes an ownership change only after issuing a permit, and the seller remains responsible for the program until then. Georgia Pre-K funding generally does not transfer, Quality Rated status does not transfer automatically, and CAPS subsidy participation requires separate approval. CACFP food-program agreements also require a new application, as described in Georgia’s change-of-ownership guidance.

Texas also treats the buyer’s permit as a gating item. A qualifying ownership change revokes the existing permit under the Texas Child Care Regulation Handbook. Texas published an expedited change-of-ownership licensing process on March 6, 2026. Confirm its eligibility requirements and current availability with Texas regulators before including the expedited process in a closing schedule.

California shows how a separate program can create its own timeline. A CACFP agreement becomes invalid when ownership changes, and the buyer must receive approval before claiming meal reimbursement. California recommends that sellers provide notice at least 60 days before the sale and that buyers apply at least 45 days before taking ownership, according to the California Department of Social Services.

Sequence the transaction around the slowest required approval. Confirm current forms, timing, and transfer rules directly with the relevant state and local agencies because requirements vary by jurisdiction and can change. Coordinate the closing plan with your M&A advisor, qualified legal counsel, and the applicable regulators.

Verify insurance coverage and finalize the adjusted EBITDA support package

Buyers will test whether your insurance program matches the center’s actual risks and contractual obligations. Gather current and historical policies, declarations, endorsements, certificates, carrier loss runs, open claim files, and insurer correspondence. Ask your insurance broker to compare coverage with state licensing rules, subsidy program terms, lease requirements, and transportation activities. Requirements vary by jurisdiction and contract.

Your center’s operations and contracts determine which policies it needs. General liability and property coverage are common, but transportation, employment, data, and abuse-related exposures may require separate policies or endorsements. Review past claims and coverage gaps that could leave the buyer responsible for an uninsured exposure. Reconcile loss runs and open claims with your incident logs and buyer disclosures.

Document the adjusted EBITDA package with the same care as the insurance file. Adjusted EBITDA starts with earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization, then accounts for expenses or income that a buyer would not expect after closing. Support every proposed add-back with invoices, payroll records, contracts, general-ledger detail, and a written explanation.

Childcare owners should pay particular attention to owner compensation and related-party rent. If you serve as the director, buyers may subtract the market cost of hiring your replacement before accepting an adjustment. If you own the property separately, support normalized rent with market evidence rather than the current lease payment. Buyers may also test personal expenses and claimed one-time costs against several years of financial records.

A quality-of-earnings review tests whether normalized EBITDA reflects earnings that are likely to continue after closing. A buyer’s accepted EBITDA may differ from the seller’s estimate after diligence, as this comparison of quality of earnings and normalized EBITDA explains. Unsupported add-backs can reduce the price, while unresolved insurance exposure can lead to an escrow holdback or a closing condition.

Preparation checklist before going to market

Enrollment and capacity

  • Reconcile enrollment, attendance, billing, and collections by classroom.
  • Separate licensed capacity from usable capacity based on rooms and staffing.
  • Document full-time-equivalent enrollment, withdrawals, and waitlist conversion.

Financials

  • Separate private-pay, subsidy, food-program, and other revenue.
  • Reconcile deferred revenue, receivables, deposits, and prepaid tuition.
  • Provide monthly financial statements and tax returns with supporting records.
  • Document each adjusted EBITDA add-back with its amount, accounting period, business purpose, and supporting records.

Staffing

  • Prepare current rosters showing roles, compensation, tenure, and schedules.
  • Confirm that director and teacher credentials remain current.
  • Organize background checks, training records, and required health files.
  • Document duties performed by the owner and identify who can assume them.

Licensing

  • Compile licenses, inspection reports, complaints, incident logs, and corrective actions.
  • Resolve open findings before buyer diligence begins.
  • Confirm change-of-ownership requirements with the applicable state and local agencies.
  • Verify separate approval procedures for subsidies and other funded programs.

Real estate

  • Review lease assignment, landlord consent, permitted use, and renewal terms.
  • Gather zoning, occupancy, and property records.
  • Address deferred maintenance, classroom condition, and playground issues.
  • Document market rent when you own the property.

Insurance

  • Assemble current policies, renewal terms, certificates, and claims history.
  • Review whether the center needs coverage for abuse and molestation claims, workers’ compensation, property, and general liability based on its operations, contracts, and jurisdiction.
  • Resolve known coverage gaps with a qualified insurance professional.
