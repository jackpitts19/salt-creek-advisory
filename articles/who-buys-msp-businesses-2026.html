Who Buys MSP Businesses in 2026?
Strategic vs. Private Equity-Backed Buyers

Operating MSPs, PE-backed platforms, and sponsors forming new platforms may value the same business differently. How to compare their cash at closing, rollover terms, client consents, and plans for your team.

An operating managed service provider (MSP), a private equity-backed MSP platform, and a financial sponsor building a new platform may assess your business differently. Compare their written payment terms, plans for clients and employees, and ability to close. No buyer category reliably pays more or closes faster.

Consider what each offer asks you to give up or keep, including control over client service and your role after closing. For broader guidance, see Salt Creek Advisory’s MSP advisory services and strategic-versus-PE buyer comparison.

Before signing exclusivity, an agreement that restricts talks with other buyers for a set period, ask how the buyer will fund the deal and which client consents it needs.

Key Takeaways
  • An unsolicited offer, or one buyer’s category, does not establish your MSP’s market value.
  • Deal structure can matter as much as headline price. Compare cash at closing with payments tied to future results and equity you would retain.
  • Contract assignment and change-of-control clauses may require client consent, which can affect closing terms.
  • When key clients depend on you personally, a buyer may lower its offer or require you to stay after the sale.
  • Get each buyer’s financing, required consents, and plans for employees and clients in writing before granting exclusivity.

Why the same MSP can get three different offers

Buyers may value the same MSP differently because each has a different plan for its contracts, staff, and service delivery. An operating MSP might already have managers and a service desk that can support your clients. A buyer building a new platform may depend more heavily on your management team remaining in place.

Revenue mix gives buyers a starting point, but it does not set the price. Recurring service contracts may appeal to a buyer more than one-time project work, but you cannot infer your MSP’s sale price by applying a separate multiple to each revenue stream. EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. Buyers assess the business’s overall profitability and test whether its recurring revenue is profitable, supported by records, and likely to continue after a sale.

Contract and customer risks can change what a buyer will pay at closing. If a major client must consent to a contract transfer or change of control, the buyer may require that consent before closing or hold back part of the price. A sale-readiness checklist explains why buyers review those clauses alongside customer concentration and founder dependence. If you personally handle major renewals, a buyer may also require you to stay or tie some payment to client retention.

A strategic buyer is an operating company acquiring another business; an operating MSP can also have private equity (PE) backing. A PE-backed platform may buy your MSP as an add-on to its existing operation, while a sponsor forming a new platform may use your MSP as its starting business. Ask what operations each buyer already has and who would serve your clients after closing.

The three types of MSP buyers

A buyer’s ownership does not tell you how it will operate your MSP. Compare who will make decisions, run client service, and fund the purchase after closing.

Operating MSP buyers

An operating MSP may buy your business to serve more customers in a region or add a capability such as security services. Because the buyer already has staff and systems, it may plan to move your clients onto its tools or combine service desks. Ask which changes will happen first and who will manage client relationships after closing.

An operating MSP can also be private equity-backed. PSP Partners lists Ntiva among its holdings, and Ntiva announced its acquisition of Site Tech Services on September 15, 2025. Ask Ntiva or any other operating buyer how it would serve your clients. Ask who must approve the deal and what company would issue any equity you retain. Confirm Ntiva’s ownership and acquisition activity again before publication.

The 20 announced its acquisition of Sundance Networks on July 6, 2026. The announcement says Sundance’s founder would continue in an AI leadership role. The announcement establishes The 20’s reported acquisition activity, not its ownership; verify its current ownership separately before publication. A founder role in one deal does not establish what another seller will be offered. Before accepting an operating buyer’s proposal, ask who will lead your employees, how client contracts will transfer, and which decisions you would still control.

Add-ons to an established PE-backed platform

An add-on sale puts your MSP inside a PE-backed company that already operates businesses in the sector. Before comparing price, ask who will supervise your managers, which decisions your staff will keep, and when the buyer plans to change your tools, brand, or client support.

PE backing alone does not tell you how quickly an MSP will integrate. Alpine Investors’ account of Wolf Consulting says the MSP continued operating independently in Pittsburgh after Evergreen acquired it in 2018, while Evergreen could centralize functions such as finance and HR. Integris announced its acquisition of TechMD, but that announcement does not establish a timetable for operational changes. Before publication, verify Integris’s current backing and acquisition activity from current sources. Ask a prospective buyer for a written plan covering reporting lines, client communications, and service delivery.

Rollover equity is an ownership stake you retain in the buyer or its parent instead of receiving that portion of the sale price in cash. Review its terms alongside the cash offer. In an add-on, your stake may sit in an existing parent company alongside other shareholders. A sponsor forming a new platform may negotiate ownership terms with you at the outset, but the value of either stake depends on its rights and the company’s future performance. Ask what class of equity you would hold, who gets paid first on a later sale, and whether future acquisitions could dilute your stake. Our strategic-versus-PE buyer comparison explains the broader rollover and platform mechanics.

Direct sponsors forming a new platform

A financial sponsor forming a new platform buys an MSP to serve as the base for future growth. Unlike an add-on buyer, the sponsor cannot point to an existing portfolio MSP’s leadership team or operating systems as the plan for your business. You need to understand who will run the company after closing, what resources the sponsor will provide, and how much responsibility you would retain.

According to Broadwing Capital’s acquisition announcement, its December 2025 acquisition of CloudScale365 launched a new MSP platform. CloudScale365 founder Patrick Hannon moved to a consulting role, the management team stayed, and Broadwing named Bret Kidd CEO. The announcement did not disclose how much cash the founder received or whether he retained an ownership stake.

The reported CloudScale365 leadership arrangement does not predict the role Broadwing Capital or another sponsor would offer you. Before you accept an offer, ask whether the sponsor expects you to lead future acquisitions or remain through a transition. If the offer includes rollover equity, ask what rights come with your stake and when you could receive cash for it.

Compare what you receive and what you retain

Start with enterprise value, the proposed value of the operating business before adjustments for debt and cash. Equity value is what remains for owners after those adjustments. Then estimate cash at closing after transaction expenses, any escrow, and any working-capital adjustment. Working capital generally refers to short-term operating assets minus short-term operating liabilities; the adjustment compares the amount delivered at closing with the amount agreed in the deal.

List an earnout, a later payment tied to agreed targets, separately from rollover equity. A higher stated price can mean less cash at closing when more of the proposed payment depends on future results.

The quality of recurring managed-services revenue affects how much of an offer a buyer will put at risk. Show account-level profitability, renewal history, and records supporting recurring billings. If a major client can terminate its contract or refuse a required consent, a buyer may seek that consent before closing or propose a holdback or retention-based payment. Salt Creek’s MSP sale-readiness checklist identifies records and contract terms to review.

Compare each buyer’s plan for you, your staff, and your clients before accepting an earnout tied to retention. If key technicians leave during a long transition, or a buyer changes service delivery and clients depart, an earnout could fall short even when you stay involved. Ask who will control staffing and client decisions, what authority you will retain, and how long the buyer expects you to work after closing.

Judge closing certainty by the work each buyer still must complete, not by its category. Ask about committed funding, internal approvals, required client consents, and due diligence, the buyer’s review of your finances, contracts, and operations. Compare proposed changes to reporting lines and service systems alongside those closing conditions.

MSP issues that can change an offer

Customer contracts can affect both the price and the closing conditions in an MSP offer. An assignment clause governs transfers of contract rights, such as those that may occur in an asset sale. A change-of-control clause can give a customer rights when ownership changes, even if your MSP remains the contracting party. Ask counsel to review the signed agreements for your largest accounts. Neither an asset sale nor a stock sale tells you on its own whether customer consent is required.

Customer concentration increases the effect of a single lost contract. If one client supplies a large share of recurring managed-services revenue, a buyer may require its consent before closing or propose terms that account for a possible departure. Review upcoming renewals and identify which customers rely on you or a particular manager to stay.

Your security practices can affect buyer fit because an acquirer must take responsibility for access to client systems. CISA’s MSP advisory explains that providers often hold privileged access across multiple customers. CISA and NSA guidance warns that attackers can misuse remote monitoring and management software, which MSPs use to administer client devices.

Prepare an inventory of administrator access and remote-access tools. Ask each buyer who will control those tools after closing and who will handle client notices and remediation costs if an incident occurs.

An escrow holds back part of the sale proceeds for defined claims after closing; unlike an earnout, its release is not necessarily tied to future business performance. Ask which claims it covers, when the funds would be released, and what could reduce the amount you receive.

Questions to ask before you sign exclusivity

Ask each buyer to put its answers in writing before you accept an exclusivity period. Once you sign, your ability to pursue another offer is restricted for the agreed term.

Cash and funding. Ask for a letter of intent (LOI), a written outline of the proposed deal terms. It should show what you would receive at closing and how the buyer plans to fund that payment.

  • How much cash would you receive after debt, transaction expenses, working-capital adjustments, and any escrow?
  • What financing and approvals remain, and what proof can the buyer provide?
  • How long would exclusivity last, and could the buyer extend it? Our letter of intent guide explains these terms in more detail.

Rollover and earnout. Find out what you can control after the sale.

  • If you retain an ownership stake, what class of equity would you receive, and what rights come with it?
  • If part of the price depends on future revenue, who controls client pricing, staffing, and how revenue gets recorded?

Staff and clients. Ask how the buyer plans to keep service steady.

  • Which employees does the buyer plan to retain, and who will manage key client relationships?
  • What role would you have after closing, and when would clients hear about the sale?

Contract consents. Review your signed agreements with counsel before assuming clients can transfer.

  • Which customer contracts require consent for an asset transfer, ownership change, or subcontracting?
  • Who will seek each required consent, and what happens if a major client delays or refuses?

Security and remote access. Establish who will protect client systems during any transition.

  • Who will control privileged client access and incident response on day one?
  • Will the buyer keep or replace your remote monitoring and management tools, and who will test service continuity before a change?

How long does an MSP sale take?

There is no fixed sale timeline. Ask the buyer for a proposed closing date and a schedule showing when it expects to finish financing, client consents, and due diligence. Review which steps must finish before closing and who is responsible for each one.
