The Working Capital
Peg

The least-understood line in your purchase agreement can hand back six figures after you've already agreed a price — and the published data says the house rules favor the house.

Direct Answer

A working capital peg is a dollar figure written into your purchase agreement that sets the amount of net working capital — receivables, inventory, and prepaid items, minus payables and accrued liabilities — you are required to deliver at closing. If the business closes with more working capital than the peg, you get paid the difference. If it closes with less, you owe the buyer the shortfall, dollar for dollar, months after the price has already been agreed and the deal has already closed. According to the American Bar Association's 2025 Private Target Mergers & Acquisitions Deal Points Study, roughly 90% of private M&A deals now include this kind of post-closing purchase price adjustment. According to SRS Acquiom's analysis of more than 1,200 private-target acquisitions, buyers' proposed working capital calculations are accepted in roughly 7 of every 10 formally contested disputes. The mechanism that decides who wins that argument is drafted months before anyone argues, and most sellers sign it without reading it as carefully as they read the multiple.

TL;DR

The peg is negotiated separately from the multiple, gets a fraction of the scrutiny, and is one of the only lines in the agreement that can still move money against you after closing.

  • 58% of deals use a separate escrow just for these adjustments, rather than relying on the general indemnity escrow (American Bar Association)
  • Buyers' proposed working capital calculations win roughly 7 of 10 contested disputes — the mechanism favors whoever drafts the first number (SRS Acquiom)

Who This Is For, and When the Peg Actually Gets Locked

This article is written for owners of businesses roughly in the $2 million to $75 million revenue range who are either preparing to go to market or are already sitting across from a letter of intent. If you are in the second group, the timing point below matters more than anything else in this article: the working capital mechanism is usually first mentioned in the letter of intent as a placeholder, often a single sentence promising a “normal level of working capital” with the details to be worked out later. Owners routinely sign that letter without pushing back, because the number itself has not been set yet and there is nothing concrete to object to. The actual peg, the reserve methodology, the collar, and the debt-like items definitions get written during the drafting of the definitive purchase agreement, which happens after the LOI is signed and after you have granted the buyer exclusivity.

That sequencing matters because your leverage is not constant across the process. Before the LOI, you can walk to another buyer. After the LOI, you are usually locked into an exclusivity period, often 60 to 90 days, during which you cannot shop the deal, and the buyer knows it. The working capital schedule gets drafted during exactly that window, which is the point in the process where a seller's negotiating position is weakest and a buyer's is strongest. Our guide on how a lower middle market sale process unfolds covers this sequencing in more detail; the short version for this article is that the traps described below are not being negotiated when you think they are.

What the Working Capital Peg Actually Is

Net working capital, for purposes of a purchase agreement, is not the same thing as the working capital line on your balance sheet. It is a negotiated, defined figure: current assets excluding cash, minus current liabilities excluding debt. Receivables, inventory, and prepaid expenses on one side; accounts payable, accrued payroll, accrued taxes, and other short-term obligations on the other. Cash and interest-bearing debt are pulled out and handled separately, through the cash-free, debt-free structure most lower middle market deals use. Exactly which accounts sit in which bucket is not a matter of accounting theory. It is a matter of what gets written into a schedule attached to the purchase agreement, and that schedule is negotiable, line by line, long before anyone signs anything.

The peg itself is the number the seller is contractually required to deliver at closing. It is almost always set as a trailing twelve-month average of the seller's own historical working capital, occasionally trailing twenty-four months, on the logic that the average represents a normal operating level the business needs to keep running without the buyer injecting new capital on day one. That logic is reasonable in principle. It becomes a problem in practice for one specific reason: the peg is calculated using an average, but the number it gets compared against on closing day is a snapshot. Averages and snapshots do not have to agree, and when they disagree, the seller pays the difference.

After closing, a closing statement is prepared showing actual net working capital as of the closing date, typically within 60 to 90 days. The buyer's team usually drafts that statement first. The seller then has a contractually defined window to review it and object, and if the two sides cannot agree, the dispute goes to an independent accountant named in the agreement, whose ruling is generally binding. If actual working capital comes in below the peg, the seller pays the buyer the shortfall, usually out of an escrow account set aside for exactly this purpose. If it comes in above the peg, the buyer pays the seller the surplus. On paper this is symmetric. In practice, the party who writes the first draft of the closing statement has a structural head start, and that party is the buyer.

The independent accountant referenced above is worth understanding on its own, because sellers tend to assume it functions like a neutral judge hearing the whole case fresh. It generally does not. The accountant's scope of review is typically limited to the specific line items the two sides could not agree on, not a full re-audit of the closing statement, which means the burden is on the seller to identify and argue each disputed item individually rather than simply pointing at a low number and asking someone to figure out why. The firm is usually named or selected by a defined process written into the purchase agreement itself — sometimes a specific regional accounting firm agreed to in advance, sometimes a process for the two sides' auditors to jointly select one if they cannot agree — and its fees are typically split between the parties or allocated based on how the ruling comes out. None of that is unusual or improper. It is simply a narrower, more procedural process than “an independent third party will sort this out fairly,” and it rewards the side that shows up with better-documented positions on each individual line item, which in practice tends to be the side with the in-house or retained deal team that does this for a living.

The buyer also has a practical advantage that has nothing to do with the contract language: by the time the closing statement is prepared, the buyer already owns and is operating the business. The buyer's team has firsthand access to the books, the staff, and the transition period; the seller is reviewing a closing statement from the outside, often without the same day-to-day access to the underlying records that they had before closing. That asymmetry compounds whatever the contract itself says about who drafts the first number.

One more mechanical piece worth understanding before you ever see a term sheet: a collar, sometimes called a deadband, is a negotiated range around the peg inside which no payment is owed by either side. A $1,000,000 peg with a plus-or-minus $50,000 collar means nothing changes hands unless actual working capital lands outside $950,000 to $1,050,000, and then only the amount beyond the collar is settled. A collar exists to keep ordinary accounting noise — a few thousand dollars of timing difference — from becoming a formal dispute. We can tell you exactly how a collar works. We cannot tell you how often lower middle market deals actually include one, because the ABA study that would answer that question is paywalled, and we were not able to independently verify a prevalence figure we would put our name on. If a collar matters to your deal, that is a question for your own deal counsel, not a statistic we are willing to invent. The equity value bridge this all sits inside — enterprise value plus cash minus debt, adjusted by the working capital true-up — is covered in more general terms in our guide to EBITDA and business valuation basics; this article is about the one piece of that bridge that keeps moving after the ink is dry.

How Common This Mechanism Is, and Where the Data Turns Soft

The scale of this is not a fringe concern. According to the American Bar Association's 2025 Private Target Mergers & Acquisitions Deal Points Study, approximately 90% of private M&A deals now include some form of post-closing purchase price adjustment, typically covering working capital, debt, transaction expenses, and cash, down slightly from 92% in the prior edition of the same study. The ABA's earlier 2023 edition separately found that among deals with an adjustment mechanism, working capital was the single most common metric, appearing in 89% of them. Those two figures come from different editions of the same recurring study and measure slightly different things — one is the prevalence of any post-closing adjustment, the other is working capital's share among the adjustment types used — and we have kept them separate here rather than blending them into one round number.

The same 2025 study reports that 58% of deals now require a separate escrow specifically for these purchase price adjustments, rather than relying on the general indemnity escrow that covers breaches of representations and warranties. That is a meaningful structural shift: buyers increasingly treat the working capital true-up as its own risk category, with its own dedicated pool of money sitting behind it, rather than as an afterthought bundled into general deal risk.

We want to be direct about the limits of what we are citing. Neither the full ABA study nor the full SRS Acquiom study discussed throughout this article is publicly available; both sit behind paywalls restricted to members or subscribers. Every figure attributed to them here comes from published summaries — law firm alerts, deal-blog write-ups, and the ABA's own announcement of its findings — not from the underlying data tables. We think those summaries are reliable enough to build a mechanism-level argument on, and we have named the publisher and linked the source every time. We do not think they are reliable enough to justify inventing a more precise number than what was actually published, and we have avoided doing that.

The Headline Number: The House Rules Favor the House

Here is the number that should matter more to you than any multiple you negotiate. According to SRS Acquiom, which analyzed more than 1,200 private-target acquisitions totaling roughly $298 billion in transaction value, buyers' proposed working capital calculations were accepted in approximately 7 of every 10 disputes that were formally contested. Read that again: this is not a measure of who is more often right. It is a measure of who wins when the two sides disagree and the disagreement gets litigated through the dispute resolution process the purchase agreement itself defines. The mechanism, as written, structurally favors the party who prepares the first draft of the closing statement, and in the overwhelming majority of deals, that party is the buyer.

The same SRS Acquiom analysis puts numbers around how much money actually sits at risk. The median separate purchase-price-adjustment escrow runs about 1% of transaction value, and that figure has been stable across the two years of data SRS Acquiom examined. Buyers' typical initial claim against that escrow runs around 0.9% of transaction value, close enough to the escrow size that the negotiating room is thin, and roughly 25% of claims exceed the full 1% escrow threshold outright, meaning the seller's exposure is not always capped by what was set aside.

There is a real, and encouraging, countertrend worth stating plainly, because the honest picture here is not uniformly bad for sellers. SRS Acquiom reported that by 2024, seller-favorable working capital surpluses — cases where actual closing working capital exceeded the peg, meaning the buyer owes the seller — had become nearly as common as buyer-favorable claims, a reversal of the historical pattern where buyer claims dominated. The share of the escrow actually claimed has also fallen sharply, from 74% in 2020 to 19% by the third quarter of 2024, which suggests deals are being negotiated with tighter, more defensible pegs than they were a few years ago. And when disputes do get contested, SRS Acquiom reports they resolve relatively quickly, in under two months on a median basis, which is a small mercy compared to the multi-month diligence timelines that precede closing in the first place.

Put together, the honest read is this: the system has gotten somewhat fairer over the past several years, and outright claims against escrow have become less common. But when a dispute is formally contested rather than settled amicably, the buyer's number wins about 70% of the time. That is the environment your peg gets negotiated into, and it is why the negotiating work described later in this article happens before signature, not after.

The Six Traps That Move the Peg After You've Signed

Category labels like “seasonality risk” or “reserve risk” are not useful to an owner staring at a closing statement. What follows is the specific arithmetic behind each trap, because knowing that reserve methodology gets scrutinized is worth nothing next to knowing exactly how a 2% versus 5% assumption on the same receivables balance turns into a five-figure hit. These mechanisms are documented individually by legal and advisory sources cited below; the way we have laid out the arithmetic in each case is our own illustration of how the mechanism plays out in dollars.

Trap One: Unmodeled Seasonality

A trailing twelve-month average smooths every month of the year into a single number. A closing date does not. If your peg is set at $800,000 using a full-year average, and your deal happens to close in January — the post-holiday low point for a seasonal business — actual working capital on that specific day might be $400,000. Nothing about the business has changed. The inventory sold through the holidays, receivables from a slower month have not yet built back up, and the cash cycle is exactly where it always is every January. But the peg does not know that, and the purchase agreement does not care why the number is low. The seller owes the buyer the full $400,000 difference. This mechanism is described by EBADAT, and it is one of the more avoidable traps on this list, because the fix does not require a different peg structure, only a better-informed one.

The fix is to model the peg against your business's actual seasonal pattern rather than accepting a flat trailing-twelve-month average by default. For a genuinely seasonal business, that means comparing the likely closing month against the same calendar month in two or three prior years, not the trailing year as a whole. If you know the deal is likely to close in a low month, that has to be reflected in the number you agree to before you sign, not discovered in the closing statement after you no longer have any leverage to change it.

This one is rarely malicious, which is exactly why it is so easy to miss. A trailing-twelve-month average is the simplest number for both sides to agree to in a letter of intent, before anyone has picked an actual closing date, and it becomes the default not because a buyer is trying to trap a seller but because nobody has a reason to complicate the math at that stage of the process. By the time a real closing date is known and the seasonal exposure becomes visible, the peg language in the definitive agreement is often already drafted around that simple average, and reopening it looks like relitigating a term everyone thought was settled. The fix has to happen while the peg is still an abstraction, not after a closing date is on the calendar.

Trap Two: Reserve Methodology Shift

Reserves — the amount set aside against receivables that will never be collected, or inventory that will never sell at full value — are calculated as a percentage of a balance, and that percentage is a judgment call. Your bookkeeper's judgment call and the buyer's post-closing accountant's judgment call do not have to match, and they routinely do not.

Say your historical bad-debt reserve runs 2% of accounts receivable, which is what your books have always used. On $500,000 of receivables, that is a $10,000 reserve. The buyer's accountants, applying a more conservative 5% assumption to the same $500,000 balance, calculate a $25,000 reserve. That $15,000 difference reduces your working capital by $15,000, with no new bad debt and no change in your actual collections — only a change in whose percentage got applied. Run the same logic on an inventory-obsolescence reserve: your historical 2% on $400,000 of inventory is $8,000; the buyer's 7% assumption on the same balance is $28,000, a $20,000 gap. Combined, those two line items alone move $35,000 in this specific example, and that is before touching warranty reserves, vacation accrual assumptions, or any other percentage-driven line in the schedule. Scale the same style of gap to the larger receivables and inventory balances typical of a $5 million business, and reserve-methodology shifts alone can commonly move $75,000 to $150,000 of adjusted working capital — a number that has nothing to do with fraud and everything to do with two accountants applying different assumptions to the same balance sheet.

The fix is to specify reserve methodology at the line-item level directly in the purchase agreement, not to leave it to be determined later by whichever accountant drafts the closing statement. Lock the aging buckets and the percentage applied to each one before closing, so the buyer's team is calculating against an agreed formula rather than substituting their own judgment for yours after the fact.

It is worth understanding why the gap opens in the first place, because it is rarely a sign of anything wrong with your books. Reserve percentages fall inside a genuinely defensible range under standard accounting practice, and a buyer's incoming auditors are frequently applying a firm-wide policy template calibrated for their broader portfolio rather than a percentage benchmarked against your specific historical write-off experience. If your reserve percentage was never tested against your actual bad-debt or obsolescence history, “that is what our books have always used” is a weak argument to bring to an independent accountant, because it describes your habit, not your risk. The stronger position is to have that benchmarking done, and the percentage justified, before the number ever becomes a negotiating point.

Trap Three: Deferred Revenue and Customer Deposits

A customer prepays $500,000 for a year of service. The seller keeps that cash at closing. The buyer inherits the obligation to deliver a full year of service against it, without the cash that was supposed to fund the delivery. If the purchase agreement does not address this explicitly, that is a $500,000 swing against the seller, because the liability to perform transfers to the buyer while the asset that was supposed to offset it does not. Lutz describes this as one of the more consistently contested items in working capital negotiations, precisely because reasonable people can disagree about whether deferred revenue belongs in working capital at all, belongs as a debt-like item instead, or should be excluded from the calculation entirely with a separate contractual mechanism covering it.

This trap is especially relevant to subscription and membership-model businesses, and it is exactly the kind of clause that needs to be nailed down in the definitions schedule before you sign anything, not discovered when a buyer's accountant applies their own default treatment to your deferred revenue balance during the true-up.

There is a bitter irony in how this one plays out. The businesses most exposed to this trap are exactly the ones growing their prepaid, recurring-revenue book fastest — the same growth a buyer will often cite as the reason for paying a premium multiple in the first place. A rapidly growing deferred revenue balance means a rapidly growing unaddressed exposure at exactly the moment a seller has the most reason to feel good about the deal. The clause that determines whether that growth is a selling point or a working capital liability is a few sentences in a definitions schedule, not a reflection of how well the business is actually doing.

Trap Four: The Cash-Free, Debt-Free Misunderstanding

The standard structure for a lower middle market deal is cash-free, debt-free: equity value equals enterprise value plus cash, minus debt, further adjusted by the working capital true-up. The trap here is definitional, not adversarial. Purchase agreements attach a schedule defining exactly which items count as “debt-like” even though they never touch a loan account — deferred revenue, unfunded pension liabilities, accrued but unpaid transaction bonuses, and similar items are common candidates. DCS Legal flags this directly: if an item such as deferred revenue gets classified as debt-like rather than as part of working capital, it can effectively be subtracted from the seller's proceeds twice — once through the debt-like items schedule at closing, and again if the same balance also depresses the working capital number used for the true-up.

Whether that happens is decided entirely by the wording of the debt-like items schedule, which is exactly why this document deserves the same scrutiny from your deal attorney as the price itself. It is genuinely purchase-agreement drafting, and we are not going to pretend otherwise or advise you on the specific language — that is a conversation for your own counsel, not your advisor.

Buyer's counsel typically drafts the first version of the debt-like items schedule, and the natural incentive on that side is to write the definition broadly, often with a catch-all phrase covering any other obligation of a similar nature not otherwise listed. A broad, open-ended definition gives the buyer's team room to characterize a newly discovered item as debt-like after the fact, even if it was never discussed during negotiations. A seller's leverage to push for a tight, fully enumerated list, with no catch-all language, is highest before the letter of intent is signed and lowest once exclusivity has begun, which is exactly backwards from when most sellers first read this schedule closely.

Trap Five: Accrued Commissions, Double-Counted

A salesperson earns a commission in December on a deal that closes and pays out in January. Ordinarily, a normal accrual for that commission is already baked into the working capital peg, because commission accruals are a routine, recurring liability. The trap appears when the same commission amount also gets pulled out and separately deducted as a debt-like item under the cash-free, debt-free schedule — effectively charging the seller for the same $50,000 obligation twice, once inside working capital and once outside it, for a $100,000 total loss on a single commission accrual. Auxo Capital Advisors describes this general double-count risk as a recurring feature of poorly reconciled debt-like items schedules, and it is easy to miss because each individual document — the working capital schedule and the debt-like items schedule — can look correct in isolation. The error only becomes visible when someone cross-references both schedules against the same trial balance line by line, which is exactly the kind of reconciliation work that belongs in diligence, not discovered after closing.

This trap is also the least likely of the five to be intentional. The working capital schedule and the debt-like items schedule are frequently drafted by different people on the buyer's side, sometimes different law firms or different teams within the same firm, working from different source documents at different points in the process. Nobody sets out to double-count a commission accrual; it happens because two schedules that were never explicitly reconciled against each other both, independently, made a reasonable-looking decision to include it. The seller pays for that lack of internal coordination either way, which is exactly why the reconciliation has to happen on the seller's side of the table, not assumed away because it looks like an oversight rather than a strategy.

A Worked Example: How the Traps Compound

We want to be explicit about what the following table is and is not. It is not a documented transaction, and it is not drawn from a single verified deal. It is a constructed illustration, built from the separately sourced mechanisms above, to show how they compound when more than one shows up in the same deal — which, in our experience, is closer to the norm than the exception once you start actually looking for them.

Take a $5 million deal with a $1,000,000 working capital peg. Layer in a $200,000 deferred revenue swing, a $50,000 double-counted accrued commission, a $75,000 reserve-methodology shift, and a $100,000 seasonality effect from a low-month closing.

An $425,000 shortfall against a $1,000,000 peg, on a $5 million deal, is 8.5% of the entire transaction value — handed back after the price was agreed, after the deal closed, and often after the seller has already mentally spent the proceeds. Again: this specific combination is constructed to demonstrate how four separately verified mechanisms compound when they occur together. It is not a statistic, it is not an average, and it is not a prediction of what will happen on your deal. It is a demonstration of why reading the working capital schedule matters as much as reading the multiple.

Run that illustration against the escrow figures cited earlier and the exposure gets sharper still. SRS Acquiom put the median separate adjustment escrow at roughly 1% of transaction value — on this $5 million illustration, that is about $50,000. A $425,000 shortfall is more than eight times that typical escrow size, meaning a seller relying on the escrow alone to cap their exposure could still owe the buyer directly, out of pocket, for whatever the escrow does not cover. Escrow size is itself a negotiated term, not a fixed feature of every deal, and it is worth asking — and having your attorney confirm in writing — whether your specific escrow is sized against a realistic worst case for your specific working capital exposures, or against a generic market convention that has nothing to do with your business's actual seasonality, reserve history, or deferred revenue balance.

How to Protect the Peg Before You Sign

Everything above points to the same conclusion: the working capital peg is negotiated once, in the purchase agreement, and almost everything that happens afterward is either the mechanical consequence of what that document says or a dispute over what it means. None of what follows is legal advice, and none of it is a substitute for your own deal attorney reading the actual language of your agreement — it is a list of the questions worth raising with that attorney before you sign, not a checklist you can complete on your own.

Model the peg against your actual seasonal pattern before you agree to a trailing-average number, using same-month comparisons across two or three prior years if your revenue moves meaningfully by season. Ask for the specific reserve methodology — the exact aging buckets and percentages applied to receivables and inventory — to be written into the purchase agreement schedule itself, not left to be determined later by whichever accountant drafts the closing statement. Get explicit written treatment for deferred revenue and customer deposits, including whether they sit inside working capital, inside the debt-like items schedule, or are addressed through a separate mechanism entirely; ambiguity here consistently resolves in the buyer's favor because the buyer's team drafts the first version of the closing statement. Cross-reference the working capital schedule against the debt-like items schedule yourself, or have your accountant do it, specifically looking for any accrual — commissions, bonuses, or otherwise — that could plausibly be counted in both places.

A working capital peg, sometimes called a working capital target, is the dollar amount of net working capital the purchase agreement requires the seller to deliver at closing. Net working capital is generally receivables, inventory, and prepaid items minus payables and accrued liabilities, with cash and debt handled separately. The peg is usually set as a trailing twelve-month average of the company's own historical working capital, on the theory that the average represents a normal operating level the buyer can rely on without injecting new capital on day one. After closing, an actual closing-date figure is calculated and compared to the peg. If your final proceeds change after the deal has already closed, it is most likely because of this line, not the multiple you negotiated.
The starting formula is current assets minus cash, minus current liabilities minus debt, though the exact accounts included and excluded are negotiated line by line and defined in a schedule attached to the purchase agreement. The peg itself is typically set by averaging that figure across the trailing twelve months, or sometimes twenty-four months, of the seller's own financial statements, using the seller's own historical accounting methods and reserve percentages. That last detail is where sellers lose money without realizing it: the peg is set using your methodology, but the actual closing-date number is frequently calculated by the buyer's accountants applying their own, more conservative methodology to the same accounts.
The true-up is the post-closing reconciliation process. The buyer typically prepares the first draft of the closing statement, usually within 60 to 90 days of closing, showing actual working capital as of the closing date. The seller then has a defined window to review and object, and unresolved disagreements go to an independent accountant named in the purchase agreement, whose decision is usually binding. If actual working capital comes in below the peg, the seller owes the buyer the shortfall, typically paid from an escrow account or directly. If it comes in above the peg, the buyer owes the seller the surplus. Because the buyer's team usually drafts the first number, the seller is negotiating from behind before the process even starts.
According to the American Bar Association's 2025 Private Target Mergers & Acquisitions Deal Points Study, roughly 90% of private M&A deals include some form of post-closing purchase price adjustment, typically covering working capital, debt, transaction expenses, and cash, down slightly from 92% in the prior edition. The ABA's 2023 edition separately found that working capital was the most common adjustment metric, appearing in 89% of deals with an adjustment mechanism. Both figures come from published summaries of the ABA study; the full study itself is paywalled, and we have not seen the underlying data directly.
Based on published analysis from SRS Acquiom covering more than 1,200 private-target acquisitions totaling roughly $298 billion, buyers' proposed working capital calculations were accepted in approximately 7 of every 10 disputes that were formally contested. That is the single most important number in this article: the mechanism is structurally tilted toward the party who drafts the first number, and that is almost always the buyer. There is a countervailing trend worth naming, too. SRS Acquiom also reported that by 2024, seller-favorable working capital surpluses had become nearly as common as buyer claims, reversing the historical pattern, even as buyer claims still won more often once a dispute was actually contested.
A collar, also called a deadband, is a negotiated range around the peg inside which no adjustment is owed by either side. If the peg is $1,000,000 and the collar is plus or minus $50,000, actual working capital anywhere between $950,000 and $1,050,000 triggers no payment at all; only the amount outside that band gets settled. A collar exists to keep small, routine accounting noise from generating a dispute over a few thousand dollars. We can explain how a collar functions mechanically, but we specifically avoid stating how often deals in the lower middle market actually include one, because the source data on prevalence is paywalled and we could not independently verify a rate. Ask your deal counsel what is customary for a transaction your size.
A peg set as a trailing twelve-month average blends every month of the year into one number, but working capital on any single closing date reflects only that day. A business with a trailing average peg of $800,000 that closes in a seasonal low month can show actual working capital of $400,000 on the closing date, even though nothing is wrong with the business, and the seller would owe the buyer the full $400,000 difference. This is not a documented industry-wide statistic; it is how the arithmetic of an averaged peg interacts with a point-in-time measurement, and it is worse the more seasonal your revenue is. The fix is to model the peg against your actual month-by-month pattern, and for seasonal businesses to compare the likely closing month against the same month in two or three prior years rather than a full-year average.
In a cash-free, debt-free structure, equity value equals enterprise value plus cash minus debt, adjusted further by the working capital true-up. The trap is definitional rather than dishonest: purchase agreements generally list specific items that count as debt-like even though they never touch a loan account, and if an item such as deferred revenue gets classified as debt-like rather than as working capital, it can effectively be subtracted from the price twice, once in the debt-like items schedule and again if it also depresses the working capital calculation. The definitions schedule in the purchase agreement, not the price you negotiated, decides which bucket an item lands in, which is exactly why this schedule belongs in front of your deal counsel and not just your broker.

First, if a customer has prepaid for services not yet delivered, the seller typically keeps that cash at closing while the buyer inherits the obligation to deliver the service, and if the purchase agreement does not address it directly, that full amount is a working capital swing against the seller. Second, if that same deferred revenue balance is separately classified as a debt-like item under a cash-free, debt-free structure rather than treated purely as working capital, it can reduce the seller's proceeds again through a different line of the same agreement.
