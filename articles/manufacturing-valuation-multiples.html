Manufacturing
Valuation Multiples

What manufacturers actually sell for depends more on who is buying than on what you make, why cash EBITDA — not the number on your P&L — is what sophisticated buyers underwrite to, and where the published data stops being trustworthy.

Direct Answer

Manufacturing valuation multiples depend heavily on which buyer universe you are reading. GF Data's Q3 2025 dataset, drawn from 211 contributor-reported transactions, puts manufacturing at 5.9x EBITDA in the $10 million to $25 million total enterprise value band, rising to 6.6x at $25 million to $50 million, 8.7x at $50 million to $100 million, and 10.0x at $100 million to $250 million — and that dataset covers private-equity-sponsored platform buyouts only. Capstone Partners, whose sample includes strategic acquirers, reports manufacturing running materially higher: 11.1x in the first half of 2025, up from 10.2x in the first half of 2024. Neither figure is wrong; they describe two different buyer populations, and strategic buyers consistently pay more than financial sponsors. The second finding matters just as much: reported EBITDA is often the wrong number to apply a multiple to in a capital-intensive business, because it ignores the equipment replacement a manufacturer cannot avoid. Cash EBITDA — reported EBITDA minus a normalized maintenance capital expenditure — is what sophisticated buyers underwrite, and the gap between the two numbers can be the difference between a good outcome and a disappointing one.

TL;DR

Two credible sources, two buyer universes, two different multiples — and neither is a lie. Which one applies to you depends on who is actually going to buy your company, and the number that multiple gets applied to matters just as much as the multiple itself.

Who The Buyer Is Moves The Multiple More Than The Sector Does

Start with the single most consequential fact in this article, because everything else builds on it. If you search for “manufacturing EBITDA multiples” you will find two credible, well-sourced figures that appear to contradict each other. GF Data, the standard reference for lower middle market transaction data, reports manufacturing multiples ranging from 5.9x to 10.0x depending on deal size. Capstone Partners reports manufacturing at 11.1x. An owner who reads one number and negotiates against a buyer quoting the other is not being shortchanged by a dishonest counterparty — they are comparing two different markets that happen to share a label.

The resolution is in the methodology, not the arithmetic. GF Data's manufacturing figures are drawn from contributor-reported transactions that are, by the source's own description, private-equity-sponsored platform buyouts. That is a specific and fairly narrow slice of the buyer universe: financial sponsors acquiring a company to hold as a platform or bolt onto one they already own. Capstone Partners' 11.1x figure covers a broader population that includes strategic acquirers — operating companies in your industry or an adjacent one, buying you because you fit into what they already do.

Strategic buyers pay more than financial sponsors as a matter of routine, and the reason is mechanical rather than a matter of generosity. A financial sponsor underwrites your standalone cash flows and a return on the equity they put in; the price they can pay is capped by what your business, run roughly as it runs today, can support. A strategic acquirer can often eliminate duplicate overhead, cross-sell into your customer relationships, absorb your production into an existing plant with spare capacity, or use your certifications to unlock a market segment they currently cannot serve. Those synergies show up as value the strategic buyer can pay for and a financial sponsor structurally cannot, which is why the same manufacturing business can be worth meaningfully different amounts to different bidders in the same process.

This is the first question to answer before any multiple means anything to you: who is actually likely to buy your company? A $6 million EBITDA machine shop with a concentrated customer base and dated equipment is a realistic target for a financial sponsor rolling up a platform, and GF Data's bands are the more relevant reference. A $6 million EBITDA aerospace supplier with AS9100 certification and a qualified position inside a larger prime's supply chain is a realistic target for that prime itself, or for a strategic consolidator already serving the same customer, and Capstone's broader figure is closer to the right neighborhood. Most manufacturing businesses in the lower middle market sit somewhere between those two poles, with both buyer types plausibly interested, which is exactly why a competitive process that tests both populations tends to produce a better outcome than negotiating with a single buyer who quotes you one side of this gap as if it were the whole market.

The practical consequence of this table is process design, not just expectation-setting. A sell-side process that only reaches out to financial sponsors is, by construction, testing against the GF Data population and is unlikely to surface the strategic premium Capstone's broader figure implies exists somewhere in the market. A process that also reaches strategic acquirers — competitors, adjacent suppliers, larger platforms already in your certified category — tests both populations at once and lets the two buyer types compete against each other for your business, rather than letting you guess in advance which one will show up. That is a structural reason competitive, broadly marketed processes tend to outperform a single unsolicited offer, independent of anything specific to your financials: an unsolicited offer, almost by definition, comes from only one side of this table.

First, the size premium is not gradual — there is a modest step from the smallest band to the next, and then a much larger jump above $50 million, which is roughly where a business becomes large enough to interest bigger institutional platforms and to support the diligence cost of a more competitive process. Second, and more important for how you read the rest of this article: every figure in that table describes private-equity-sponsored platform buyouts. If your business is realistically a target only for financial sponsors — because of scale, customer mix, or the absence of the kind of certification or capability a strategic would specifically want — this ladder is close to your market. If your business could plausibly attract a strategic acquirer, this ladder is a floor rather than a ceiling.

Cash EBITDA: The Number Sophisticated Buyers Actually Underwrite

The buyer-universe question decides which multiple applies to you. This second question decides what that multiple gets applied to, and it is the one owners in capital-intensive businesses most often get wrong, usually because no one has told them there are two different EBITDA figures worth calculating.

Reported EBITDA — earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization — adds back depreciation because it is a non-cash accounting entry. That treatment makes sense for a services business with light fixed assets. It makes considerably less sense for a manufacturer, because depreciation exists in the first place to spread the real cash cost of equipment over its useful life, and equipment in a manufacturing plant genuinely wears out and genuinely needs to be replaced. Reported EBITDA adds that cost back to earnings and then stops, as if the machine will simply keep running forever. It will not, and a buyer who has underwritten manufacturing deals before knows it will not.

Cash EBITDA is the corrective. It is calculated as reported EBITDA minus a normalized, multi-year average of maintenance capital expenditure — the capital spent specifically to keep existing equipment and facilities running, as distinct from growth capex spent to add new capacity. We use a three-year average because maintenance capex is lumpy by nature; a single year with a major press rebuild or a roof replacement will distort a one-year snapshot in either direction, and averaging smooths that out into something closer to a steady-state run rate.

Work through the arithmetic on a concrete example, because the gap between the two numbers is larger than most owners expect. A manufacturing business reports $3 million of EBITDA on $30 million of revenue. Its maintenance capex has averaged 5% of revenue over the last three years — $1.5 million a year, a reasonable figure for general manufacturing. Cash EBITDA is $3 million minus $1.5 million, or $1.5 million. Now apply a multiple to each number. A buyer offering “6x” against the reported $3 million produces an enterprise value of $18 million. The same 6x applied to the $1.5 million of cash EBITDA produces $9 million. Both offers can be honestly described as “6x EBITDA” on a term sheet, and they are $9 million apart in what actually lands in the seller's account.

The same $3 million reported EBITDA and $30 million of revenue produces a materially different cash EBITDA depending on where your actual maintenance capex falls within the general manufacturing range described later in this article. The table below holds revenue and reported EBITDA constant and varies only the capex assumption, to show how sensitive the underlying number is to a fact about your business that a headline multiple never captures:

Maintenance Capex (% of Revenue) Maintenance Capex ($) Cash EBITDA Enterprise Value at 6x Cash EBITDA
4%$1.2M$1.8M$10.8M
5%$1.5M$1.5M$9.0M

All three rows describe a business with identical reported EBITDA of $3 million and identical revenue of $30 million. The only variable moving is maintenance capex intensity, and it swings enterprise value at a constant 6x from $10.8 million to $5.4 million — a factor of two, entirely inside the general manufacturing range of 4% to 7% we cite later in this article. This is why a buyer's first substantive financial question in a manufacturing process is almost always about your capex history rather than your revenue growth: it is doing more work in the valuation than almost anything else on your income statement.

That is not a hypothetical trap set by a bad-faith buyer. It is what happens by default when a business with meaningful maintenance capex gets valued on the wrong denominator, and it is exactly the kind of adjustment a private equity buyer or a well-advised strategic will make in their own model whether or not you present it first. The practical implication is straightforward: know your three-year average maintenance capex, know your cash EBITDA, and understand which number is actually driving the offer you are looking at before you compare it to a published multiple. How Much Maintenance Capex Should You Budget?

If cash EBITDA is the number that matters, the maintenance capex figure that produces it matters just as much, and it varies meaningfully by the kind of manufacturing you do. The commonly used industry underwriting frameworks are:

Sub-Sector Maintenance Capex (% of Revenue)
General manufacturing4% – 7%
Machine shops3% – 5%
EMS / contract manufacturing3% – 5% (light capex, heavy inventory)

We want to be precise about what these figures are and are not. They are industry underwriting frameworks — the kind of ranges a buyer's finance team uses to sanity-check whether a business's stated maintenance capex is plausible, or to build an estimate when a seller's historical capex data is thin. They are not the product of a survey of actual manufacturing transactions, and we have not located transaction evidence that ties a specific capex-to-revenue ratio to a specific, quantified compression in multiple. Some sources present a rule of thumb along those lines; we are not willing to repeat a precise compression figure as fact when we cannot trace it to transaction data, and we would rather tell you that plainly than dress up a framework as a finding.

The EMS and contract manufacturing note is worth expanding on, because it illustrates that capex intensity is not the whole capital story. Contract manufacturers typically run lighter fixed-asset bases than, say, a metal stamper running heavy presses, but they often carry substantial working capital in raw materials and finished goods inventory to meet customer delivery schedules. A buyer evaluating a contract manufacturer's cash generation has to look at both maintenance capex and working capital intensity together; a business that looks capital-light on the capex line alone can still tie up significant cash in inventory, and that cash is unavailable to the buyer just as surely as capex spending is.

The practical use of this table is as a starting comparison, not a verdict. Pull your actual three-year maintenance capex history, divide by revenue, and see where you land relative to your sub-sector's range. If you are meaningfully above it, understand why — older equipment, a recent catch-up cycle, a genuinely capital-intensive process — because a buyer will ask the same question, and “I don't know” is a worse answer than an honest explanation.

The Certification Mechanism: Why AS9100, NADCAP, and ISO 13485 Move the Multiple

Look across the broker-published sub-sector ranges later in this article and a pattern emerges immediately: the highest multiples cluster around certifications. Aerospace shops holding AS9100 or NADCAP run well above general precision machining. Medical device manufacturers holding ISO 13485 or FDA registration run well above general contract manufacturing. This is the most useful analytical point in this article after the buyer-universe question, because understanding the mechanism — not just observing the correlation — tells you what is actually worth investing in.

The mechanism is not that certified work is technically harder, though it often is. It is that a certification like AS9100, NADCAP, or ISO 13485 functions as a barrier to entry on both sides of the relationship. It takes real time, real money, and a real audit history for a competitor to earn the certification your shop already holds, which limits how many suppliers can even bid on the work. And it takes real time, real money, and real risk for your customer to requalify a new supplier once they have already qualified you — a new supplier audit, a first-article inspection process, a validation run, and in regulated industries, sometimes a regulatory filing tied to the specific approved source. That combination is what economists would call a switching cost, and it is a switching cost that sits on your customer, not on you.

A buyer is not paying a premium for the certificate on your wall. They are paying for the fact that your customer relationship is expensive for a competitor to take away, which means the earnings underlying that relationship are more durable than the earnings of an uncertified shop competing on price against anyone with a comparable machine. That durability is exactly what a multiple is supposed to price, which is why certification correlates with multiple as consistently as it does across every certified category in the sub-sector data below.

The implication for an owner is concrete. If you are weighing whether to pursue AS9100, NADCAP, or ISO 13485 certification ahead of a sale, the honest framing is not “will this look good to a buyer.” It is “does this make it more expensive for my customers to leave and more expensive for a competitor to take my place.” If the answer is yes and the certification is achievable in your planning window, it is one of the few investments in this article with a clear, mechanism-based case for paying off in the multiple, distinct from anything to do with tariffs or reshoring, where no such case currently exists in the data. A few patterns are worth drawing out. The certification effect described above is visible directly in the numbers: aerospace and medical device categories, both defined by regulatory or industry certification, sit meaningfully above general fabrication and machining categories that compete more directly on price and capacity. Scale matters within a sub-sector as much as it does across the GF Data bands — aerospace, metal stamping, and contract manufacturing all show a higher range once EBITDA clears a threshold, generally in the $10 million to $15 million area, which tends to coincide with the point where a business is large enough to be a credible platform rather than a single bolt-on. And food and beverage sits apart from the rest of the table entirely, with premium beverage's 12x to 18x range reflecting brand equity and consumer product economics that have little to do with the machining or fabrication logic driving the rest of the list; we include it here because it is part of the broker's manufacturing category, not because the value drivers are the same.

Precision Machining and Aerospace: Where Certification Does the Most Work

The spread inside this single family of processes is the clearest illustration of the certification mechanism in the entire table. General precision machining at 5x to 8x and AS9100/NADCAP aerospace work at 7x to 10x can describe two shops running comparable equipment, comparable headcount, and comparable revenue — the difference is almost entirely the audit trail and the qualified-supplier status the certification represents. The step up again above $25 million of EBITDA in the aerospace category, to 8x to 12x, reflects scale layered on top of certification: a business large enough to be a credible sole-source or dual-source platform for a prime contractor, not just a qualified job shop bidding on individual work packages.

Medical Device: The Highest Regulatory Bar, the Highest Multiples

Medical device manufacturing carries the highest broker-published ranges of any process category in this table, and the regulatory bar is correspondingly the highest. ISO 13485 and FDA registration are not one-time achievements; they require an ongoing quality management system, documented design controls, and a manufacturer's own audit history to survive a customer's supplier qualification and, in many cases, the FDA's own inspection regime. The jump to 10x to 13x for Class III implantable device manufacturers specifically reflects the fact that Class III devices carry the FDA's most rigorous premarket approval pathway, which raises the switching cost for a customer to the point where requalifying a new supplier can be a multi-year undertaking rather than a multi-month one.

Metal Fabrication, Stamping, and Injection Molding: Commodity Process Economics

General metal fabrication, metal stamping, and general injection molding sit at the lower end of the table, running roughly 4x to 7.5x depending on scale. These are processes without an industry-standard certification comparable to AS9100 or ISO 13485 gating the customer relationship, and competition tends to run more directly on price, capacity, tooling investment, and delivery performance rather than on a barrier a competitor cannot easily cross. The step up for medical or aerospace-grade injection molding, to 7x to 10x, is the certification mechanism reappearing inside a category that is otherwise commodity-priced: the molding process itself does not change, but the qualification standing behind a medical or aerospace customer relationship does.

Contract Manufacturing and EMS: Capital-Light, Inventory-Heavy

Contract manufacturing and electronics manufacturing services run 5x to 8x depending on scale, a step above general fabrication but below the certified aerospace and medical categories. As discussed above in the maintenance capex section, this category's lighter fixed-asset base is often offset by heavier working capital tied up in materials and finished goods inventory, which is why a buyer evaluating an EMS business has to look past the capex line to the full cash conversion cycle before concluding the business is genuinely capital-light.

Specialty Food and Beverage: A Different Value Driver Entirely

Food and beverage manufacturing multiples run from 5x for general specialty food up to 18x for premium beverage, a wider spread than anywhere else in the table, and the reason has little to do with the mechanisms driving the rest of this article. A differentiated bakery brand or a premium beverage label is priced substantially on brand equity, category growth, and distribution reach — consumer products economics closer to what drives a branded goods acquisition than what drives a certified aerospace supplier acquisition. We include the category because it is part of the broker's manufacturing classification, and because some owners in adjacent processed-food manufacturing will find it a useful reference point, but the certification and capex frameworks elsewhere in this article are a poor guide to what moves value in this specific slice of the table.

Customer Concentration: The Other Lever Buyers Actually Price

Certification protects a customer relationship from being taken by a competitor. Customer concentration measures how much of your business depends on that relationship continuing to exist at all, and it is priced separately and, in some cases, more aggressively than any other single factor in this article.

CT Acquisitions' broker-published guidance describes revenue under 10% of total from any single customer as supporting full multiples, while concentration above 30% from a single customer draws a 30% to 40% haircut. The mechanism is simple risk transfer: a buyer paying a multiple of your EBITDA is implicitly betting that EBITDA continues, and the more of it depends on one relationship, the more the entire valuation depends on a single customer's purchasing decision rather than on the durability of your operation as a whole. A haircut of 30% to 40% on the multiple, applied to a business at, say, 6x, is not a rounding adjustment — it can be the difference between a 6x and a 4x outcome, which on meaningful EBITDA is a difference measured in millions of dollars.

Manufacturing has a specific wrinkle on customer concentration that is worth naming. The same switching costs that make a certified relationship valuable to you also mean that losing a concentrated customer can be genuinely sudden and hard to backfill — there is often no comparable customer waiting to absorb qualified capacity on short notice, particularly in aerospace and medical device work where a new customer relationship can take a year or more to fully qualify. That is different from a services business, where a lost client can sometimes be replaced within a sales cycle. Buyers underwriting manufacturing concentration risk know this, which is part of why the haircut can run as steep as it does. This pattern is not unique to manufacturing — our guide on what buyers look for in an acquisition target covers how concentration is priced across the lower middle market generally — but the qualification timeline specific to certified manufacturing work makes it worth flagging on its own.

The practical preparation, if concentration is a known issue in your business, is the same advice that applies everywhere else it comes up: start diversifying the customer base well before a sale process, document why the relationship is durable if it genuinely is (a long qualification history, multi-year purchase agreements, sole-source status), and expect the conversation to happen regardless, because a buyer's diligence team will find the concentration in your financials whether or not you raise it first.

What Else Buyers Underwrite in a Manufacturing Deal

Cash EBITDA, certification, and customer concentration are the three factors that move a manufacturing multiple the most, and they are the ones we can attach the clearest mechanism to. A serious buyer's diligence goes further, and it is worth naming the additional areas plainly rather than implying the three above are the whole story.

Equipment condition and remaining useful life. Maintenance capex tells a buyer how much cash a business has been spending to keep equipment running; it does not by itself tell them how much equipment life is left, or whether a wave of replacement is coming regardless of the historical average. A buyer's technical diligence in a manufacturing deal typically includes a walkthrough of major equipment with an eye toward age, condition, and remaining life, and a business whose equipment is approaching the end of its useful life across the board presents a different risk than one with a steady replacement cadence, even if the historical capex percentages look identical on paper.

Environmental, health, and safety exposure. Manufacturing carries environmental and safety liability that lighter-asset businesses generally do not: permitting, waste handling, chemical storage, and workplace safety records among them. A clean EHS record and current permits are not typically a source of valuation upside, but a poor one is a source of real downside — unresolved violations, contamination history, or a weak safety record can slow a process down, trigger specific indemnification demands, or in a serious case affect price. This is diligence territory rather than multiple-driver territory, and it belongs on an owner's preparation checklist for that reason.

Backlog and order visibility. A manufacturer with a documented, quotable backlog gives a buyer near-term revenue visibility that a business selling purely against a sales forecast does not. This matters more in some sub-sectors than others — aerospace and defense work often comes with multi-year program visibility, while some contract manufacturing runs closer to purchase-order-to-purchase-order — but wherever it exists, a documented backlog is worth organizing clearly for a buyer rather than leaving it scattered across purchase orders and verbal commitments.

Workforce depth and key-person risk. Skilled trades — machinists, welders, certified quality inspectors — are frequently harder to hire than the equipment is to buy, and a business where critical process knowledge sits with one or two long-tenured employees rather than being documented and cross-trained presents a real transition risk a buyer will want addressed, structurally or through price, before closing.

None of these four factors has a clean, quantifiable multiple effect the way the buyer-universe and certification mechanisms do, which is why they are not headline sections in this article. They are, however, exactly the kind of detail a quality-of-earnings and operational diligence process surfaces, and preparing documentation on each before you go to market tends to shorten a process rather than lengthen it. Our guide to quality of earnings reports covers how a formal diligence engagement tests these areas alongside the financial adjustments discussed earlier in this article.

Do Tariffs and Reshoring Move the Multiple?

This question comes up in nearly every manufacturing conversation we have right now, and we want to answer it as honestly as we answered the buyer-universe question above: there is a real narrative, and there is no source we have found that puts a defensible number on it.

The narrative itself is not manufactured out of nothing. Reshoring, nearshoring, and supply chain diversification away from single-country dependence are widely cited as tailwinds behind rising manufacturing deal activity, and Capstone Partners' own multiple increase from 10.2x in the first half of 2024 to 11.1x in the first half of 2025 is sometimes discussed in that context. Deal activity in domestic manufacturing does appear to have risen over that period. That much we are comfortable stating.

What we are not comfortable stating is a specific effect on your multiple, because we have not found a source that isolates tariff or reshoring exposure as a variable and quantifies what it is worth in turns of EBITDA. It would be easy to write a sentence like “domestic manufacturers with tariff-exposed import competition command a premium of X turns,” and it would be exactly the kind of confident-sounding, unsupported claim that erodes trust once an owner or a buyer goes looking for the source and cannot find it. We would rather leave this section shorter and honest than longer and wrong.

If your business genuinely benefits from reshoring dynamics — import-competing products, customers actively diversifying their supply base toward domestic sources, defense or critical-infrastructure exposure — that story belongs in your data room and your management presentation as qualitative context a buyer should weigh. It is a legitimate part of the case for your business. It is just not, at present, a number we can hand you.

Owned vs. Leased Real Estate: What the Data Does and Doesn't Support

Manufacturing is real-estate-intensive by nature, and a meaningful share of the owners we talk to own their facility, frequently through a separate entity from the operating business. It is a fair question whether that ownership itself increases what you get for the business, and here again we want to be precise rather than reassuring.

We have not found data supporting the claim that owned real estate commands a valuation premium over leased real estate in a manufacturing sale. That may be counterintuitive — owning your building feels like it should be worth something — but the operating business and the real estate are, in most transactions, priced as two separate things rather than one blended asset. The operating company is valued on a multiple of its EBITDA, and if you own the building and charge the operating company below-market or no rent, a buyer will typically impute a market rent before applying that multiple, similar to how a below-market rent arrangement gets corrected in other real-estate-heavy sectors. The real estate itself then gets valued separately, on its own basis, whether it is sold to the buyer outright, retained and leased to the buyer under a new arm's-length lease, or handled through a sale-leaseback executed around the time of the transaction.

That last structure, the sale-leaseback, is worth understanding on its own terms rather than as a source of premium. In a sale-leaseback, the owner sells the real estate to a third-party investor (or the buyer retains it and leases it back to the operating company, or a separate real estate buyer is brought in alongside the operating business buyer) and the operating company signs a new lease to continue occupying the facility. It is a legitimate way to monetize real estate value separately from operating business value and can be useful for structuring total proceeds, but it is a transaction mechanism, not evidence that ownership itself lifts your multiple. If you own your facility, the more useful question to bring to an advisor is not “does this raise my multiple,” but “how should the real estate and the operating business be structured and priced separately to maximize total proceeds,” which is a different and more answerable question.

Who This Article Is For

This article is for you if you own a manufacturing business and want to understand where it sits in the market before you talk to an advisor. It builds on Salt Creek's guide to M&A advisors for manufacturing and industrials companies, which covers the buyer landscape and process, and goes deeper on valuation mechanics specifically. Most owners we work with run founder-owned or family-owned companies with roughly $2 million to $75 million in revenue and generally at least $500,000 of adjusted EBITDA. Within that range, the GF Data bands and the certification and concentration mechanics described above apply directly. If your business is well below that range, your likely buyer is an individual operator or a small strategic rather than a private equity platform or a larger strategic consolidator, and the top of the sub-sector ranges above is not realistically your market. Treat what follows as a map for calibrating expectations, not an appraisal of your specific company.

How Manufacturing Multiples Compare Across the Lower Middle Market

Manufacturing sits in an unusual position relative to other sectors we cover: its financial-sponsor multiples run at or below the broader lower middle market average, while its strategic-inclusive multiples run above it, which is itself a restatement of the buyer-universe finding driving this entire article.

  • Against the broader lower middle market on a like-for-like basis. GF Data's cross-industry averages sit in a comparable range to its manufacturing-specific bands at similar deal sizes, which tells you manufacturing is not unusually discounted within the private-equity buyer population once you control for size. Our guide to EBITDA and valuation basics covers those cross-industry brackets directly.
  • Against recurring-revenue services. The contrast with managed IT services is instructive precisely because it isolates a different value driver. An MSP trades at a premium substantially because its revenue is contracted and assignable on paper; a manufacturer's customer relationship, even a certified and long-tenured one, is durable in practice but rarely contractual in the same sense.   • Against businesses with lighter capital intensity. The cash-EBITDA gap described above is largely specific to capital-intensive sectors; a professional services or business services company with minimal fixed assets does not face the same reported-versus-cash-EBITDA divergence. That is worth knowing if you are benchmarking your manufacturing multiple against a friend's services business multiple — the two numbers are not adjusted on the same basis even when they look similar on paper.

Reported EBITDA is the number brokers quote and multiples get published against, but it is frequently the wrong number for a sophisticated buyer to apply a multiple to, because it deliberately ignores the equipment replacement a manufacturer cannot avoid. Cash EBITDA — reported EBITDA minus a normalized, multi-year average of maintenance capital expenditure — is closer to what a buyer is actually underwriting to when they run their own model, because it reflects earnings after the capital a plant needs just to keep running, not the capital that grows it. A business with heavy machinery, frequent tooling replacement, or an aging plant can show flattering reported EBITDA while its cash EBITDA is far lower, and a buyer who has done this before will find that gap during diligence whether or not you present it yourself. The honest move is to run both numbers before you go to market, so you know which conversation you are actually walking into.
Cash EBITDA equals reported EBITDA minus a three-year average of maintenance capital expenditure — the capital spent to keep existing equipment and facilities running, as distinct from growth capex spent to add capacity. Work through a concrete example: a business reports $3 million of EBITDA on $30 million of revenue, and maintenance capex has averaged 5% of revenue over the last three years, or $1.5 million a year. Cash EBITDA is $3 million minus $1.5 million, or $1.5 million. Apply a 6x multiple to the reported $3 million and you get $18 million of enterprise value. Apply the same 6x to the $1.5 million of cash EBITDA and you get $9 million. Both are '6x' by the label on the term sheet, and they describe a $9 million difference in what the seller actually receives. A buyer underwriting seriously will ask for the capex history and run this math themselves, so the number is worth knowing before you are told it across the table.
The commonly used underwriting frameworks put general manufacturing at roughly 4% to 7% of revenue, machine shops at 3% to 5%, and EMS or contract manufacturing at 3% to 5% — the last of those sometimes described as light on capex but heavy on inventory, since contract manufacturers tend to carry working capital burden in materials rather than fixed assets. We want to be direct about what these figures are: industry underwriting frameworks used to sanity-check a business's numbers, not results from a survey of actual transactions, and we have not found transaction evidence establishing a precise relationship between a specific capex ratio and a specific multiple. Treat them as a starting range to compare your own three-year maintenance capex average against, not as a rule that determines your price.
Broker-published ranges consistently show certified manufacturers commanding higher multiples than uncertified ones in the same process category — aerospace shops holding AS9100 or NADCAP certification at 7x to 10x EBITDA versus general precision machining at 5x to 8x, and medical device manufacturers holding ISO 13485 or FDA registration at 8x to 12x. The more useful finding is the mechanism behind that gap, not just its size. A certification like AS9100, NADCAP, or ISO 13485 is a barrier to entry: it takes real time and money for a competitor to earn, and it takes real time and money for your customer to requalify a new supplier once they have qualified you. That switching cost is what a buyer is actually paying for. The certification itself does not make the machining better; it makes the customer relationship expensive to move, which is the thing that protects the earnings a multiple is applied to.
Broker-published guidance from CT Acquisitions describes revenue under 10% from any single customer as supporting full multiples, while concentration above 30% from one customer draws a 30% to 40% haircut. The logic is straightforward: a buyer underwriting your earnings is underwriting the risk that your largest customer leaves, and the higher the share of revenue tied to one relationship, the more of your EBITDA disappears if that relationship ends. This is not unique to manufacturing — buyers price concentration risk the same way across the lower middle market — but it shows up with particular force in manufacturing because switching a qualified supplier can be genuinely difficult for a customer, which cuts both ways: it protects you while the relationship holds and exposes you if it does not.
