Rollover Equity Explained:
What Your Second Bite Is Really Worth (2026)

A private equity buyer asks you to keep part of the price in the business. The percentage is the easy part. The class of equity, the terms above it, and how long you wait decide what the stake ever pays you.

TL;DR
  • Rollover equity is part of your sale price kept as ownership. You reinvest it in the buyer’s holding company instead of taking cash, and it pays out when the private equity firm later sells. Goodwin Procter found that 57% of US mid-market deals used it in 2023, up from 46% in 2020, as reported by Preqin.
  • 20% to 30% is typical. That is William Blair’s estimate for middle-market deals, and larger businesses typically roll less. Check whether the percentage is of the price, your proceeds, or the new company.
  • The class of equity matters more than the percentage. In our hypothetical example, the same $6 million rollover returns $13.5 million or $9.3 million in a strong exit, and $3.0 million or nothing in a weak one, depending only on whether the sponsor holds participating preferred equity.
  • Expect to wait about six years. Put rights to sell early are very rare, per William Blair, and Private Equity Info puts the median holding period for 2025 exits at 5.9 years.
  • It is tax-deferred, not tax-free. Gain on the cash you take is generally taxed at closing, and tax on the deferred gain generally comes due when you later sell the stake. Equity tied to staying employed can be taxed as compensation.
  • Negotiate before exclusivity. The same class as the sponsor, fair-value leaver terms, drag-along and tag-along rights, and information rights are easiest to win while other buyers are still at the table.

Rollover equity is the part of a business sale price that the seller reinvests in the buyer’s new holding company instead of taking in cash at closing. In a private equity deal, an owner selling a company for $30 million might take $24 million in cash and roll $6 million into equity, keeping a minority stake that pays out when the private equity firm (the “sponsor”) later sells. That later payout is the “second bite of the apple.” Whether it is worth more or less than the cash you gave up depends on the class of equity you receive, the debt and preferred claims ahead of it, the rights in the equity documents, and how long the sponsor holds the business.

What is rollover equity?

In a rollover, you sell your company to a private equity buyer but reinvest part of the price in the entity the buyer forms to own it, usually a new holding company. You remain a shareholder, now a minority one, alongside the sponsor and often alongside your management team. The rest of the price arrives as cash at closing, less debt repayment, escrow, and any working capital adjustment.

Buyers ask for rollover for practical reasons. It reduces the cash and debt they need at closing, keeps the person who knows the business invested in its next few years, and can bridge a gap between what you want and what they will pay in cash today. Goodwin Procter attributed the rise in rollovers to “a climate of economic uncertainty and challenging debt-financing markets.”

The second bite is real, but it is not guaranteed. Rolled equity is a new investment in a business you no longer control, usually carrying more debt than you ran it with, under documents the sponsor drafted. Porte Brown frames the decision well: the key question is not simply “How much am I rolling?” but “What exactly am I receiving in return?”

The published figures cluster around 10% to 30% of the deal, with founders who stay on often rolling more. None of the sources below publishes a breakdown for companies with $5 million to $75 million in revenue, so treat them as orientation rather than a benchmark for your deal.

The holding-period trend matters most for planning. A stake you expected to cash in after four or five years may now take six or more, and the median hides a wide spread: some sponsors sell early, and some hold far longer while they wait for a better market.

Is a 20% rollover 20% of the company?

“Roll 20%” can mean 20% of the purchase price, 20% of your after-tax proceeds, or 20% of the new company’s equity. They produce different numbers because the buyer funds part of the purchase with debt, which sits ahead of every shareholder.

Take a hypothetical company sold for $30 million. The sponsor borrows $10 million and invests $14 million of its own money, and you roll $6 million. Your rollover is 20% of the price, but the new company has only $20 million of equity, so your $6 million buys 30% of it. A larger ownership share sounds better, but it also means your stake carries the leverage: if the company loses value, the lenders are repaid first and the loss falls on the equity. As Porte Brown puts it, “A 20% rollover of sale proceeds is not the same as 20% post-closing ownership.” Ask for the post-closing capitalization table, which lists every class of equity and the debt ahead of it, before you agree to a percentage.

A hypothetical worked example: same rollover, different outcomes

Continue the example. You hold $6 million of equity, 30% of the company, and the sponsor holds $14 million. Several years later the sponsor sells, and after the debt is repaid the equity is worth $45 million in a strong exit, $20 million in a flat one, or $10 million in a weak one. The table compares two structures. In the first, you hold the same class of equity as the sponsor. In the second, the sponsor holds participating preferred equity: it recovers its $14 million first, then shares in what remains in proportion to ownership. The figures are illustrative, not typical terms or a Salt Creek transaction, and they ignore fees, taxes, and management incentive equity.

Equity value at exit, after debtSame class as the sponsor (you own 30%)Sponsor holds $14M participating preferred; you hold common
Strong: $45M$13.5M$9.3M
Flat: $20M$6.0M$1.8M
Weak: $10M$3.0M$0

In the strong case, the same-class stake returns $13.5 million, 30% of $45 million. Under the preferred structure, the sponsor first takes $14 million and you receive 30% of the remaining $31 million, or $9.3 million. In the flat case, where the equity is worth exactly what was invested, the same-class stake returns your $6 million, while the preferred structure leaves you $1.8 million. In the weak case, the preference absorbs everything. The business performs identically in both columns. The only difference is one term in the equity documents.

Real terms vary. A preference may be non-participating, meaning the sponsor takes either its preference or its share as if converted to common, whichever is larger. In our example, a non-participating preference would leave you the same $13.5 million and $6.0 million as the same-class column in the strong and flat exits, because converting to its 70% share would pay the sponsor at least as much, but you would still receive nothing in the weak one. It may accrue a fixed annual return, or cover only part of the sponsor’s investment. Ask your counsel to run the actual waterfall, the order in which exit proceeds are paid, at several exit values, including one below the price you sold at.

The terms that decide what your stake is worth

The rollover percentage appears in the letter of intent. Most of what decides its value is settled later, in the holding company’s operating agreement or stockholders agreement. Porte Brown’s guide covers most of the terms below; put rights come from William Blair, and affiliate transactions and amendment protection from Lathrop GPM. Raise them before you sign the letter of intent, while you still have other buyers.

Two of these deserve extra attention. Dilution: issuing new equity at fair value to fund an add-on lowers your percentage without necessarily lowering your stake’s value, but a management incentive pool or equity issued below value does reduce it. Leaver provisions: read the definition of “cause” closely, because it decides which repurchase price applies. William Blair also notes that many rollover transactions include vesting schedules based on time and performance, so ask whether any equity you receive vests and what happens to the unvested part if you leave. William Blair also notes that most rollover agreements come with non-compete, non-solicit, and non-hire agreements, which limit what you can do while you wait.

How is rollover equity taxed?

Rollover is usually structured so that the rolled portion is not taxed at closing, while the cash portion is. Porter Hedges explains that when the rollover equity is an interest in an entity taxed as a partnership, Section 721 of the Internal Revenue Code governs it, and deferral is relatively easy to achieve. When the buyer’s holding company is a corporation, the rollover usually relies on Section 351, which has an extra hurdle: the people contributing property must together hold at least 80% of the voting power and 80% of the total number of shares of all other classes of stock immediately after the exchange. Koley Jessen notes that the sponsor’s cash counts toward that test but stock issued solely for services does not, and that if service equity pushes the group below 80%, the rollover may fail to qualify for deferral. If your company is itself a corporation, a tax-free reorganization under Section 368 can sometimes be used instead. Porter Hedges adds that partnership holding companies are very common for financial buyers, and corporate ones less so.

Deferral is not forgiveness. You generally carry your old tax basis into the new equity, so the gain you did not pay tax on at closing stays built into the stake, and tax on it generally comes due when you later sell the stake in a taxable sale. Structure involves tradeoffs for the buyer too: Koley Jessen notes that in C corporation acquisitions, seller tax deferral and the buyer’s step-up in asset basis “rarely travel together.” A buyer that wants the step-up may propose a structure that changes your tax result. Two more points can change your result. First, vesting: if your rolled equity is tied to staying employed or can be forfeited, Porte Brown warns that some or all of it may be treated as compensation rather than investment equity, which can mean ordinary income tax. Where equity vests, an 83(b) election can sometimes lock in the tax treatment at closing, but it generally must be filed within 30 days of the transfer and a missed deadline cannot be fixed afterward. Because you paid for rolled equity with your own sale proceeds, ask that it be fully vested at closing. Second, qualified small business stock: if your company is a C corporation, ask your tax advisor whether your stock qualifies under Section 1202, which can exclude some or all of the federal gain on a sale. Porte Brown explains that stock received in a Section 351 or 368 exchange can keep that status, but the benefit does not carry into a partnership or LLC rollover under Section 721, and Koley Jessen notes that contributing qualifying stock to a partnership holding company disqualifies it from the future exclusion. Some states do not follow the federal rules. Bring your tax advisor in before the letter of intent fixes the structure. How is rollover equity different from an earnout, seller note, or escrow?

Rollover is one of several ways part of the price moves out of the closing wire. They carry different risks, so compare offers with the same headline price by what each component depends on.

Rollover in a platform deal versus an add-on

Where your equity sits changes what you are betting on. In a platform deal, the sponsor’s first acquisition in a sector, your rollover is usually an investment in the company you know, and you may help run it. In an add-on, your stake usually sits in the larger parent company alongside other founders and the sponsor, and its value depends on businesses you have never seen and debt you did not negotiate. Ask for the parent’s financial statements and debt levels before treating an add-on rollover as equivalent to a platform one. The tax deferral can be harder to get too. Porter Hedges notes that when the rollover is into a corporation, the 80% test can be relatively easy to meet in a platform deal but harder in an add-on, and Lathrop GPM notes that in an add-on a rollover may not be possible, or at a minimum will be more complex to work out. Our strategic-versus-PE buyer comparison and roll-up guide explain how platforms and add-ons are built.

How much equity should you roll over?

  1. Start from the cash you need. Work out what you need at closing, after tax, for your own plans. Roll only what you could afford to lose, because the weak case in the example above is a real outcome.
  2. Get the capitalization table. Ask the buyer for the post-closing debt, every class of equity, and any management pool, and convert the rollover into an ownership percentage.
  3. Model the waterfall. Have counsel or your advisor run the actual terms at a strong, flat, and weak exit value, and over a six-year hold as well as a four-year one.
  4. Compare against all-cash offers. A higher headline price with a junior rollover can be worth less than a lower price with a same-class stake or no rollover. Competing offers are the only way to know what an all-cash price would be.
  5. Check the sponsor as a partner. Your stake depends on how well the sponsor runs the business and how it treats minority holders. Lathrop GPM advises reviewing the firm’s track record and its reputation for dealing with minority investors, and making sure the people behind its past results are still active at the firm. Ask to speak with founders who rolled equity with this sponsor, including one whose company did not do well.
  6. Negotiate terms before exclusivity. Class of equity, leaver provisions, and drag and tag rights are easiest to win while other buyers are still at the table.

Frequently asked questions

What is rollover equity in a business sale? It is the part of the sale price you reinvest in the buyer’s holding company instead of taking as cash. You keep a minority ownership stake that is usually paid out when the buyer later sells the company.

Is rollover equity a good deal for sellers? It can be, if the business grows under the new owner and you hold equity on the same terms as the sponsor. It is also a new investment with leverage, limited control, and no set exit date, so compare it with taking the same amount in cash.

How much rollover equity is typical? William Blair estimates 20% to 30% for middle-market deals, and Porte Brown cites 10% to 30%, sometimes higher for founders. Check whether the figure is a share of the price, of your proceeds, or of the new company’s equity.

Can I refuse to roll over equity? Yes, but some buyers will lower their price, add an earnout, or move on, because rollover reduces the cash they need and keeps you invested. Running a process with several buyers shows which will pay all cash and at what price.

When do I get paid for my rollover equity? Usually when the sponsor sells or recapitalizes the company. Private Equity Info puts the median holding period for 2025 exits at 5.9 years, and William Blair describes put rights that let you sell earlier as very rare.

What is the second bite of the apple? It is the payout from your rollover equity when the private equity buyer later sells the company. The first bite is the cash you receive at closing. The second can be worth more or less than what you rolled, depending on the company’s value at exit, the debt and any preferred equity ahead of you, and how long the sponsor holds it.

Is rollover equity taxable? Usually not at closing, if it is structured under Section 721 or Section 351 of the Internal Revenue Code. It is tax-deferred, not tax-free: gain on the cash portion is generally taxed at closing, and tax on the deferred gain generally comes due when the stake is later sold. Equity tied to staying employed can be taxed as compensation instead.

What happens to my rollover equity if I leave the company? It depends on the repurchase and leaver provisions. A good-leaver clause may let the company buy your stake at fair market value, while a bad-leaver clause may set the price at cost or at a discount.

Can my rollover stake be diluted? Yes. New equity issued for add-on acquisitions or to reward management lowers your percentage. Issuance at fair value need not lower your stake’s value, but a management incentive pool or equity issued below value does. Preemptive rights let you invest to keep your share.

Is rollover in an add-on the same as in a platform deal? No. In an add-on, your stake usually sits in the larger parent company, so its value depends on the whole platform and its debt, not only on the business you sold.

What is the difference between rollover equity and an earnout? An earnout is a capped later payment tied to targets the business must hit. Rollover equity is ownership: it has no cap, but it can fall to zero, and it pays only when the company is sold.
