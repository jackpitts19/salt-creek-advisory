Choosing representation for a commercial landscaping sale

A landscaping business broker may specialize in residential lawn routes rather than larger commercial contracts. Ask for named commercial maintenance, snow, or design-build sales and confirm whether the advisor represented the seller. A service page or broker directory cannot establish that record on its own.

Your revenue mix affects which buyers an advisor should approach and what information they need. Buyers may examine commercial maintenance contracts for recurring revenue. They may assess snow work for weather-related variation and design-build work for its project backlog and scheduling. If a few property managers account for much of your revenue, ask how the advisor will protect their identities during buyer outreach.

CCG Advisors

CCG Advisors is a sector-specialist option for commercial landscaping owners who want an advisor that reports named sell-side transactions in the sector. CCG says it advised on the 2020 sale of Cutting Edge Property Maintenance to BrightView. Cutting Edge provided commercial snow removal, landscape maintenance, and related services. CCG also says it served as exclusive advisor on the 2023 sale of Moore Landscapes to Yellowstone Landscape. CCG’s transaction list provides additional landscaping assignments to examine. Check each entry for the client represented and the services the seller provided.

CCG says its typical clients generate $10 million to $300 million in revenue. That stated range makes CCG worth considering if your company is near or above $10 million in revenue, but it may be a fit issue for a smaller operator. Calder Capital

Calder Capital is a potential fit if your company serves both commercial and residential customers and you want an advisor with documented Michigan landscaping and snow assignments. Calder says it exclusively represented Camp Lawn Care & Snowplowing, which served commercial and select residential clients. It also says it exclusively represented Wolverine Lawn, which served residential and commercial customers and provided snow removal. The cited announcements do not provide sale prices or seller revenue.

Calder names Jon Pastoor as lead advisor on Camp and Scott Nicholson on Wolverine. Those assignments show who led the reported deals, not who would lead yours. Before signing, ask Calder who will handle buyer outreach, how it will protect customer and employee confidentiality, and what total fees you would pay.

One distinction matters when assessing Calder’s sell-side record. Calder advised the buyer in the Boulder Landscaping transaction, not Boulder as the seller.

Keystone Business Advisors

Keystone Business Advisors is a regional option for California commercial landscape maintenance owners, particularly those serving homeowners associations (HOAs). In a transaction Keystone reported in September 2022, the firm said it represented CTR Landscape Services, a Ventura County HOA maintenance contractor, in a sale to a local strategic buyer. Keystone did not disclose the sale price or CTR’s revenue.

Keystone says it typically markets landscape companies with more than $500,000 in EBITDA to strategic buyers, or companies seeking an acquisition, and private equity firms, or investment firms that buy businesses. EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. It measures operating earnings, not the cash an owner receives at closing. Keystone says it approaches companies below that threshold differently, so ask how it would market yours.

Keystone also says its advisors handle no more than five clients each at a time, but that limit does not guarantee which advisor will lead your sale. Its seller FAQ says the firm collects a fee only after a completed transaction and requires buyer confidentiality agreements before sharing confidential information. Ask who will manage your engagement and confirm the total fee in writing.

Harris Williams

Harris Williams is worth considering if your commercial landscaping company is large enough for a sale process like the Yellowstone Landscape transaction described below. Harris Williams says it advised Yellowstone Landscape on its 2019 sale to Harvest Partners. At the time, Yellowstone served more than 5,000 customers through 40 branches. Harris Williams also reports advising Yellowstone on a minority investment, not another full-company sale.

Yellowstone’s scale makes Harris Williams useful context for owners with far larger businesses than Salt Creek’s typical clients, who generate $2 million to $75 million in revenue. If your company falls within that range, ask Harris Williams whether it would take your engagement and who would handle the work.

Questions to ask before hiring a landscaping business broker

Before you sign an engagement letter with any advisor, including Salt Creek Advisory, get written answers on buyer outreach, confidentiality, staffing, and total cost.

  • Which buyers would you approach for a company with our mix of commercial maintenance, snow, and design-build work? How would you identify them, and when would you contact them?
  • How would you protect our identity during outreach? Ask when buyers must sign confidentiality agreements, what information they receive at each stage, and who approves its release.
  • Who would prepare our materials, contact buyers, and handle negotiations day to day? Ask which senior advisors will stay involved after you sign.
  • What would we pay if a sale closes, if we end the engagement, or if a buyer introduced during the engagement closes later? Request the proposed success fee, any retainer or deposit, expense charges, and any payment obligations after termination.

Put the agreed staffing, disclosure rules, fees, and post-termination obligations in the engagement letter or an attached plan. Verbal answers may not settle what the advisor must do or what you owe.

FAQs

  • How does a commercial landscaping broker differ from a residential lawn-route broker? A commercial advisor should be able to explain how buyers assess maintenance contracts, reliance on a few major customers, snow revenue, and design-build work. A residential route broker may focus instead on how many customers can be served along a route and on individual customer accounts. Ask each advisor for relevant completed sales, not just a landscaping listing.
  • What does a success fee mean? It is a fee due when a sale closes, usually calculated under the formula in your engagement letter. A success-fee arrangement can still include a deposit, expenses, or payment obligations after termination, so check those terms separately.
  • When might a regional specialist be the better choice? Consider one when your likely buyers operate locally or your contracts depend heavily on regional relationships. Ask which buyers the advisor would approach and how the advisor would protect your identity.
  • Should an owner under $2 million in revenue hire a broker or wait? Ask brokers who regularly sell companies below $2 million in revenue whether your business fits their process and what their minimum fee would be. You can also ask an advisor what preparation would make a later sale easier. Do not assume that crossing $2 million will, by itself, change your options.

To the extent applicable, our M&A engagements are conducted in reliance on the federal M&A broker exemption at Section 15(b)(13) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 (15 U.S.C. § 78o(b)(13)), added by the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023 and effective March 29, 2023. Under that provision, “an M&A broker shall be exempt from registration” when it effects securities transactions solely in connection with the transfer of ownership of an “eligible privately held company” and reasonably believes the buyer will control the business and be active in its management afterward. An eligible privately held company is one with no class of securities registered with, or reporting to, the SEC that, in the fiscal year before the engagement, had EBITDA of less than $25,000,000 or gross revenues of less than $250,000,000 (amounts subject to periodic inflation adjustment). The exemption is not available to a broker that receives, holds, transmits, or has custody of the funds or securities being exchanged; acts for an issuer in a public offering; acts in a transaction involving a shell company; provides financing, or helps arrange third-party financing without complying with applicable law and disclosing its compensation in writing; represents both buyer and seller without clear written disclosure and the written consent of both; facilitates a transaction with a group of buyers it helped form; transfers ownership to a passive buyer; or binds a party to the transaction. Where a transaction would fall outside those conditions, we do not rely on the exemption, and any services that only a registered broker-dealer may perform would have to be provided by a registered broker-dealer, not by us. The federal exemption does not displace state law, and some states separately regulate business brokers and M&A intermediaries.
