Preparing to Sell September 17, 2026 By Jack Pitts, Co-Founder

How to Prepare a
Dog Daycare Business for Sale in 2026

Owners preparing a dog daycare for sale in 2026 should begin 12 to 24 months before going to market. Buyers assess the history behind adjusted earnings, management independence, customer activity, legal compliance, and facility condition. The final three to six months should focus on confirming those records and resolving issues that could delay diligence or change the deal terms.

TL;DR
  • Prepare your dog daycare for sale in two phases. During the first phase, build a documented history of consistent financial reporting, stable staffing, complete records, and operations that do not rely on the owner.
  • Financial cleanup and operating changes need months to produce a credible track record. Starting late leaves less time to resolve buyer concerns, which can lead to lower accepted adjusted EBITDA, a longer transition, or more purchase price tied to future performance.
  • During the final three to six months, you should verify adjusted EBITDA support, update records, review licenses and lease terms, and address facility issues before diligence begins.

Clean up financials and build adjusted EBITDA support

Buyers often recalculate adjusted earnings because internal financial statements are usually prepared to manage the business and support tax reporting, not to substantiate a sale price. They compare tax returns with financial statements, then trace unusual expenses through the general ledger. Unexplained differences can weaken confidence in the earnings claim.

EBITDA measures earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. Adjusted EBITDA adds or subtracts items that a buyer does not expect to continue under new ownership. For a dog daycare, boarding facility, or pet resort, proposed adjustments may include excess owner compensation or a documented expense from a one-time legal dispute. Pet-care valuation guidance also explains why owner-held real estate requires a fair-market rent adjustment.

Owner compensation usually needs more analysis than a simple add-back. An adjusted EBITDA schedule may add back your current compensation and then deduct the market cost of replacing the duties you perform. Keep payroll records and write a clear description of your daily responsibilities. Compensation surveys, recruiter estimates, or recent hiring data can help support the cost of replacing your duties.

Buyers are more likely to accept a one-time expense adjustment when records establish its amount and explain why the expense will not recur. Keep invoices, engagement letters, and maintenance records for each claimed adjustment. Buyers may treat an expense as recurring when similar charges appear in several years, even if your financial statements label each charge as unusual.

Related-party rent and personal expenses face more scrutiny. Support rent adjustments with comparable leases, a broker opinion, or an appraisal. For vehicle costs, travel, insurance, and other owner expenses, separate personal use from legitimate business use through receipts and tax-return detail. A buyer may reject the entire adjustment when the records do not establish the personal portion.

A quality-of-earnings review tests the accuracy and persistence of the earnings presented to buyers. A CPA firm typically reconciles several years of records and requests transaction-level support for adjustments. The scope and cost of a quality-of-earnings review vary with the size of the business, the condition of its records, and the number of adjustments under review. Buyers may reduce or reject adjustments that lack transaction-level support, as explained in this guidance on EBITDA add-backs.

Start 12 to 24 months before a sale by closing the books consistently, reconciling accounts, and retaining support for each adjustment. During the final three to six months, update the schedule through the latest reporting period and confirm that every number ties to the underlying records. Buyers are more likely to accept a limited schedule supported by transaction records than a larger schedule based on estimates.

Reduce owner and manager dependence

Buyers treat daily owner involvement as an operating risk because revenue and service quality may change after the owner leaves. If you approve schedules, resolve customer complaints, or manage every serious pet incident, buyers cannot tell whether current earnings will continue under new ownership. A buyer may respond with a lower valuation, a longer transition requirement, or an earnout that makes part of the purchase price contingent on future performance.

Begin transferring authority 12 to 24 months before a sale. Give the general manager written authority over staffing, customer resolutions, purchasing, and safety decisions within defined limits. Document recurring procedures in a location employees can access, and assign a backup for each function needed to keep the daycare operating.

Proof of delegation comes from operating records rather than an organizational chart. Scheduling logs should show managers approving shifts, while incident records should identify who investigated and resolved each event. Booking and operating dashboards can give managers and owners daily access to revenue, utilization, and staffing information without requiring the founder to assemble each update. Buyers can review those records to confirm that employees consistently use the procedures.

Dependence on one general manager creates another form of key-person risk. Cross-train supervisors, centralize passwords and vendor contacts, and make sure more than one employee understands capacity planning and emergency protocols. Before going to market, step away for several extended periods and record which decisions still return to you. Use each escalation to revise a procedure or clarify a manager’s authority before buyers assess management independence during due diligence.

Stabilize staffing and fix worker classification

Stable staffing gives buyers evidence that the daycare can maintain service after ownership changes. Prepare a monthly employee roster that tracks hire dates, departures, roles, compensation, training, and scheduled hours. Buyers will compare this information with payroll and operating schedules to identify turnover, understaffing, or dependence on a few experienced employees. Documented training and clear job responsibilities make recent staffing improvements more credible.

Worker classification deserves review well before diligence begins. The IRS common-law test examines behavioral control, financial control, and the working relationship. A groomer may qualify as an employee when the facility controls the schedule and provides the equipment, even if the business issues a Form 1099. Misclassification can create exposure for unpaid employment taxes.

The Labor Department applies a separate economic-reality test for minimum wage and overtime obligations. Its worker-classification guidance considers control, worker investment, permanence, and whether the service forms an integral part of the business. A signed contractor agreement does not determine status under that test. Worker-classification standards can change and may differ under federal and state law. Employment counsel should review the rules in effect before you change classifications or approach buyers.

Organized employment records help buyer counsel assess whether liabilities may carry into the transaction. Keep payroll records, time records, offer letters, handbook acknowledgments, training records, disciplinary documentation, and termination paperwork in consistent files. Retention periods vary by record type and governing law. Ask employment counsel to confirm the federal and state requirements that apply before you discard personnel records.

Form I-9 records need separate attention. Employers must retain forms for current employees and, after separation, for three years after hire or one year after employment ends, whichever comes later, under USCIS retention rules. Review files early enough to correct permitted technical errors rather than discovering missing records during buyer diligence.

Organize customer, membership, and pet records

Buyers test membership revenue by comparing billings with cash receipts, attendance, and services delivered. Differences may indicate failed collections, unused credits, timing errors, or deferred-revenue obligations. Pet-services subscription accounting guidance explains how recognizing revenue before the service is delivered can overstate current earnings and understate deferred revenue.

Utilization also affects the economics of an unlimited plan. A $425 membership produces about $35 per visit when a dog attends 12 days per month. At 18 days, revenue falls to about $24 per visit, and total labor and supply costs may rise with the additional visits. Buyers therefore review visit frequency by member and location rather than accepting enrollment totals as proof of recurring, profitable revenue.

Renewal records show whether membership revenue can continue after a sale. For plans with a defined renewal point, calculate churn by dividing the number that did not renew by the number eligible for renewal during the period. If 225 of 250 eligible plans renew, churn equals 10%, based on the standard renewal calculation. You should track renewals by monthly or annual cohort because blended enrollment can hide losses among existing customers.

Before going to market, reconcile each membership and prepaid package across your booking, payment, and accounting systems. Maintain a customer-level ledger showing charges, check-ins, unused visits, refunds, and deferred revenue. Document how you estimate unused prepaid balances. If the records do not reconcile, buyers may recalculate revenue and adjusted EBITDA, treat unused packages as a liability, or reject the claim that the revenue recurs predictably.

Complete pet and customer files help buyers verify compliance with admission requirements and determine whether the customer database contains usable records. A buyer will sample active accounts for current vaccination records, signed waivers, intake forms, temperament evaluations, and emergency treatment authorizations. A system that stores vaccine expirations, intake documents, and digitally signed waivers in each account can make missing or expired records easier to identify.

Standardized agreements document admission requirements, treatment authorization, and each party’s responsibilities if an animal is injured or becomes ill. This daycare and boarding agreement provides one example, but local counsel should review your form for the laws that apply to your facility. Confirm that every active customer signed the current approved form and that your CRM can export complete customer and pet records for a buyer.

Confirm lease transfer, licensing, and zoning requirements

A valid lease and current animal-care license do not establish that the buyer may operate after closing. Buyers need evidence that the lease, permitted use, licenses, and zoning will remain effective or can be approved for the new owner without interrupting operations.

Review the lease before approaching buyers. An asset sale generally changes the tenant and requires a lease assignment, while an equity sale may leave the tenant entity unchanged. However, commercial leases often treat a change in ownership as a deemed assignment that requires landlord consent. Missing consent can create a default, delay closing, or affect renewal options and other negotiated rights. Commercial lease guidance explains why owners should review assignment, subletting, and change-of-control provisions early.

Buyers also examine the remaining lease term, renewal options, permitted use, guaranties, rent obligations, and landlord approval standards. A landlord may evaluate the buyer’s finances and operating experience before granting consent. Transaction structure can therefore affect both the consent requirement and the time needed to obtain approval, as described in this business-sale lease overview.

An estoppel certificate documents key lease facts at a specific point in time. The certificate may confirm that the lease remains in force, identify amendments, state the rent and expiration date, and disclose known defaults or disputes. Buyers use the certificate to compare the written lease with the parties’ actual understanding. Legal guidance on estoppel certificates explains how buyers and lenders use the tenant’s certified statements about the lease and any existing defaults.

Animal-facility licensing requires a separate review. Iowa expressly states that animal welfare licenses are non-transferable, and a new owner must submit an application and pass an inspection before operating. Iowa also directs applicants to check local zoning and animal-count restrictions. Iowa’s Animal Welfare FAQ provides the applicable requirements.

North Carolina follows a different administrative approach. The state requires boarding-kennel licenses, provides a change-of-ownership form, and requires notice when ownership or management changes. North Carolina’s licensing materials show why owners should confirm whether a sale requires notice, a new application, an inspection, or another approval.

Iowa and North Carolina are illustrative examples, not nationwide rules. Ask local counsel and the relevant state and municipal agencies to confirm lease consent, licensing, zoning, and closing requirements for your facility.

Document safety, incidents, and insurance coverage

Buyers value complete and consistent incident reporting more than an implausible claim that no problems occurred. Buyers expect occasional incidents in a pet-care operation. They focus on whether you documented each event, notified the owner, followed established procedures, addressed the cause, and reported any related insurance claim.

Assemble bite and injury reports, escape records, disease outbreaks, veterinary correspondence, customer complaints, insurance claims, and any animal-control notices. Match each incident to follow-up actions and policy changes. You should also maintain vaccination records, behavioral notes, staff training records, and incident follow-ups as part of your operational safety documentation. Missing or inconsistent records make it harder for a buyer to determine whether past events created unresolved liabilities.

Buyers also examine disease-control procedures. Keep written vaccination requirements, cleaning schedules, isolation protocols, outbreak communications, and records showing who completed each task. Staff files should document training in canine behavior, safe group-play intervention, emergency handling, evacuation procedures, and incident escalation. Signed attendance sheets or system records provide stronger support than an undated training manual.

Your insurance review should confirm that the policies match the services you provide and the number of animals you may have onsite. Standard commercial general liability policies typically exclude property in the insured’s care, custody, or control. Because the law generally treats pets as personal property, general liability may not cover an animal that becomes injured, sick, lost, or killed while under your supervision. Care, custody, and control coverage, which may be offered through animal bailee coverage or an endorsement, can address this common coverage gap.

Organize current policies, prior policy periods, certificates, endorsements, loss runs, claim correspondence, and renewal notices. Confirm that coverage applies to group play, overnight boarding, transportation, and maximum capacity where relevant. A missing endorsement or unexplained claim can require additional insurance review, delay diligence, or affect the buyer’s proposed price and deal terms. A complete insurance file allows the buyer to compare reported incidents with claims, coverage terms, and the actions you took in response.

Assess facility condition and deferred maintenance

Buyers evaluate facility condition because near-term repairs can reduce cash available after closing or interrupt operations. A pet-care inspection may examine HVAC and ventilation, drainage, flooring, plumbing, electrical service, security, sanitation equipment, and play areas. Pet-care facility guidance explains how ventilation, drainage, flooring, and sound control affect animal care and daily operations.

Buyers assess usable capacity by comparing peak occupancy with the space available for safe separation, cleaning, feeding, and movement rather than relying on the number of kennels or runs. Damaged enclosures, poor drainage, or inadequate isolation space can make part of the stated capacity impractical.

A Facility Condition Index can help you estimate the maintenance burden before an inspection. The index divides estimated deferred maintenance cost by the facility’s current replacement value. For example, $150,000 of deferred maintenance against a $3 million replacement value produces a 5% index. CoreNet Global’s FCI guidance describes the calculation and common condition ranges. Pet-care owners should use the index as a screening tool, not as a valuation rule.

Begin 12 to 24 months before a sale by listing major assets, recording age and condition, and obtaining planning estimates for material repairs. Prioritize safety concerns and items that could interrupt service. If you lease the property, separate landlord obligations from tenant obligations and preserve related notices and correspondence.

During the final three to six months, update the list and assemble invoices, warranties, inspection reports, maintenance logs, and contractor estimates. A buyer or lender may order a property condition assessment that reviews major systems, identifies deficiencies, and estimates repair needs. Commercial property assessment guidance describes how these reviews support acquisition and financing decisions.

Deferred maintenance can remain unresolved at closing when buyers can verify its scope, cost, and effect on operations. Written estimates, inspection reports, and maintenance records allow buyers to estimate the cost and operating effect of known issues. Unclear or undisclosed issues create uncertainty and may affect underwriting, disclosures, transaction timing, or negotiated terms, depending on their cost and operational impact.

Build the track record buyers will actually trust

Buyers give recent improvements less credit because those changes have not yet proved durable. A price increase may raise revenue for one month but later reduce customer retention. A staffing fix may lower overtime temporarily without resolving turnover. Buyers want actual operating results that show the improvement can continue after the sale.

When recent changes contribute to adjusted EBITDA, buyers test whether the accounting records support those gains and whether the gains are likely to continue under new ownership. Buyers separate documented historical results from pro forma assumptions based on expected savings or future revenue. Windes identifies future savings included in the asking price before they materialize as a diligence warning sign.

Start major changes 12 to 24 months before a sale so you can build evidence over time. For a price increase, track retention and revenue after the effective date. For a staffing change, document payroll savings and stable service levels. Monthly records carry more weight than a run-rate calculation based on a few weeks of performance.

Weak support can affect both valuation and payment terms. A buyer may reduce the adjusted EBITDA used for valuation or protect against uncertainty through an escrow or earnout. Quality of earnings findings can change the earnings base and deal structure, so proven gains have a better chance of supporting cash paid at closing.

Frequently asked questions

Can I sell an owner-dependent business?

You can sell an owner-dependent business, but buyers will usually identify the dependence through employee interviews, approval workflows, customer relationships, and operating records. Buyers may request a longer transition period, an earnout, or other protections when the owner remains central to daily operations.

How far in advance should I engage an M&A advisor?

Consider speaking with an advisor 12 to 24 months before a possible sale. Early advice can identify staffing, lease, licensing, and documentation issues while you still have time to correct them. Salt Creek’s pet-care advisory overview explains its approach to founder-owned businesses.

What happens if a license cannot transfer to the buyer?

The buyer may need to obtain a new license before closing. Depending on local rules and the purchase agreement, the parties may delay closing, make approval a closing condition, or revise the transaction structure. Consult local counsel and the responsible licensing agency early.

How can I estimate what my dog daycare may be worth?

Buyers consider adjusted earnings, management depth, recurring customer activity, facility condition, lease terms, and growth capacity. Salt Creek’s dog daycare and pet-care valuation guide provides range context, but no industry multiple determines a specific company’s value.

Should I fix every facility issue before going to market?

Address safety, compliance, and business interruption risks first. For other deferred maintenance, obtain estimates and disclose the work clearly so buyers can assess the cost without assuming a larger problem.

Decide when to involve an advisor

An M&A advisor can review unsupported add-backs, lease restrictions, incomplete records, and owner dependence while you still have time to address them. The advisor can then prioritize the issues most likely to change adjusted EBITDA, delay diligence, or require buyer protections in the purchase agreement.
